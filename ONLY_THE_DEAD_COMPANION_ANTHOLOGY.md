### INTRODUCTION TO VERDUN

The Battle of Verdun lasted 303 days. From February 21 to December 18, 1916, French and German armies fought for control of a few kilometers of ruined earth in northeastern France. When it ended, approximately 714,000 men were casualties. The front lines returned to roughly where they had begun.

This book exists to help you understand that battle, that place, and what it meant to be there.

Verdun was not a battle of dramatic charges or tactical brilliance. It was a battle of endurance. Artillery fired an estimated 40 to 60 million shells over those 303 days—an average of 130,000 shells per day. The opening bombardment on February 21, 1916, delivered one million shells in nine hours. Seventy to seventy-five percent of all casualties at Verdun were caused by artillery, not rifles or machine guns. Death came from above, randomly, constantly.

The German plan was attritional: bleed France white by forcing them to defend an emotionally significant but strategically limited position. The French responded with a rotation system that cycled 70 of their 96 divisions through Verdun. Nearly the entire French Army experienced Verdun. It became a defining trauma of French national identity.

This was industrial warfare at its most elemental: earth pulverized, forests obliterated, villages erased. The Zone Rouge—the Red Zone—remains restricted today due to unexploded ordnance and contaminated soil. Nine villages were never rebuilt. Their legal status is "Mort pour la France"—Died for France.

### WHY THIS BATTLE MATTERS

Verdun matters because it represents the intersection of modern industrial capacity and human endurance. It was a test of how much suffering a nation could sustain. The answer was: more than anyone thought possible, but at a cost no one fully understood until afterward.

For players of *Only The Dead*, understanding Verdun is essential because the game does not simplify or dramatize. You will experience survival, not heroism. The systems you encounter—hunger, thirst, artillery recognition, trench maintenance, disease management—are grounded in documented reality. This book explains the historical basis for those systems.

### WHAT MADE VERDUN DIFFERENT

**Scale**: The concentration of artillery exceeded anything in military history to that point.

**Duration**: 303 days of continuous combat with no operational pause.

**Terrain**: The battlefield became physically uninhabitable—a moonscape of craters, mud, and decomposition.

**Rotation**: The French noria system meant most of the army experienced Verdun personally.

**Futility**: The strategic outcome was essentially a stalemate, but the psychological impact was permanent.

# SECTION I — HISTORICAL GROUNDWORK

This section explains how the First World War began, why Verdun became its most infamous battle, and what made it different from all other engagements. The information here is factual, verified from historical archives, and presented without embellishment.

---

## CHAPTER 1: ORIGINS OF THE FIRST WORLD WAR

### The Long Peace and the Cascade

Europe had not seen a general war between major powers since 1815. Nearly a century of relative peace had fostered economic integration, dynastic marriages, and technological advancement. By 1914, the continent was more interconnected than ever before—and more heavily armed.

The war began with an assassination. On June 28, 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary and his wife Sophie were killed in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb nationalist. Austria-Hungary issued an ultimatum to Serbia. Serbia accepted most terms but not all. Austria-Hungary declared war on July 28, 1914.

What followed was a cascade of alliance obligations and military mobilizations:

- **Austria-Hungary** declared war on Serbia.
- **Russia** mobilized to defend Serbia (Slavic solidarity and Balkan influence).
- **Germany** declared war on Russia (August 1) to support Austria-Hungary.
- **France** mobilized under its alliance with Russia.
- **Germany** declared war on France (August 3) and invaded Belgium to outflank French defenses.
- **Britain** declared war on Germany (August 4) due to treaty obligations to Belgium.

By mid-August 1914, Europe was at war. What military planners expected to be a short, decisive conflict—"home by Christmas"—became a stalemate that lasted four years.

### Why the War Became a Stalemate

**Defensive Technology Outpaced Offensive Tactics**

Machine guns, barbed wire, and entrenchments made frontal assaults suicidal. Attackers advanced across open ground into concentrated fire. Artillery could destroy defenses, but infantry could not exploit the damage before defenders reinforced.

By late 1914, the Western Front solidified into parallel trench systems running from the English Channel to the Swiss border—approximately 700 kilometers of fortified lines. Neither side could break through.

**Attrition Became Strategy**

Unable to achieve breakthrough, commanders turned to attrition: inflict more casualties than the enemy could sustain. Victory would come not from maneuver but from exhaustion. This logic led directly to Verdun.

### The Western Front by 1916

By February 1916, the war had been static for over a year. The French and British had launched costly offensives in 1915 (Artois, Champagne) with minimal gains. German commanders sought a way to break the deadlock without repeating the same failures.

The answer, conceived by German Chief of Staff Erich von Falkenhayn, was Operation Gericht—Operation Judgment. The target: Verdun.

---

## CHAPTER 2: WHY VERDUN WAS CHOSEN

### German Strategic Calculus

Erich von Falkenhayn believed that breaking French morale was more achievable than breaking French lines. His plan was not to capture Verdun and exploit a breakthrough, but to force France into a battle of attrition where German artillery superiority would "bleed France white."

Verdun was chosen for specific reasons:

**1. Symbolic Value**

Verdun was historically and emotionally significant to France. The fortress city had withstood sieges during the Franco-Prussian War (1870) and medieval conflicts. Losing Verdun would be a national humiliation. Falkenhayn believed France would commit unlimited resources to defend it.

**2. Salient Geography**

Verdun sat in a salient—a bulge in the French line, surrounded on three sides by German positions. This allowed German artillery to fire from multiple directions, concentrating firepower on a limited area.

**3. Limited Supply Routes**

Verdun had only one major supply route: a secondary road connecting Verdun to Bar-le-Duc, 75 kilometers southwest. Falkenhayn believed French logistics would collapse under sustained pressure. (He underestimated French determination to maintain this lifeline, which became La Voie Sacrée—The Sacred Way.)

**4. Fortress System**

The ring of French forts around Verdun (Douaumont, Vaux, Souville, and others) had been partially disarmed in 1915, as French High Command believed modern artillery made fortresses obsolete. Falkenhayn thought the forts would fall quickly, creating panic.

**5. Artillery Concentration**

The German Army could concentrate unprecedented artillery firepower on a narrow front. Falkenhayn massed 1,220 guns along a 20-kilometer front—one gun every 16 meters. The opening bombardment would be the heaviest in history.

### French Strategic Position

France did not choose to fight at Verdun; they were forced to respond. Once the battle began, political and emotional imperatives made withdrawal impossible.

**Joseph Joffre**, French Commander-in-Chief, initially underestimated the threat. By the time German intentions were clear, reinforcements were already en route.

**Philippe Pétain**, appointed to command the Verdun sector on February 26, declared: "Ils ne passeront pas"—They shall not pass. This became the battle's defining slogan.

French strategy became defensive endurance: hold the line, rotate divisions to prevent collapse, and outlast German offensive capacity. It worked, but at staggering cost.

### The Miscalculation

Falkenhayn's plan assumed France would suffer disproportionate casualties due to German artillery dominance. This proved false. German casualties were nearly equal to French losses. The attrition was mutual.

By mid-1916, with the Battle of the Somme drawing German reserves westward, Verdun had become a German strategic failure. But by then, both sides were committed. The battle continued for ten months.

---

## CHAPTER 3: STRATEGIC GOALS OF FRANCE AND GERMANY

### German Objectives

**Primary Goal: Attrition**

Falkenhayn's Christmas 1915 memorandum to Kaiser Wilhelm II outlined the strategy: force France into a battle where German artillery would inflict unsustainable casualties. The goal was not territorial gain but the destruction of French military capacity and national will.

**Secondary Goal: Capture Verdun**

If French defenses collapsed, German forces would take the city. This would shorten German lines and create a propaganda victory.

**Tertiary Goal: Divert Allied Resources**

By fixing French forces at Verdun, Germany hoped to prevent French participation in planned Allied offensives elsewhere.

### German Execution

The operation began with overwhelming force. On February 21, 1916, German artillery fired one million shells in nine hours, followed by infantry advances using flamethrowers and storm tactics. Initial gains were rapid—Fort Douaumont fell on February 25 with minimal resistance.

But German advances slowed as French reinforcements arrived and artillery parity was achieved. By April, Germany extended attacks to the left (west) bank of the Meuse to silence French artillery positions on Mort-Homme and Hill 304. This widened the battle and increased German casualties.

By June 23, German forces reached their farthest advance—within four kilometers of Verdun city. Crown Prince Wilhelm requested permission for a final assault. Falkenhayn refused. German resources were exhausted. The Battle of the Somme, launched July 1, forced reallocation of German reserves.

The offensive ended. Attrition had been mutual. The strategic goal failed.

### French Objectives

**Primary Goal: Hold Verdun**

Withdrawal was politically and symbolically impossible. France would defend Verdun regardless of cost.

**Secondary Goal: Minimize Casualties**

Pétain's rotation system (the "noria") cycled divisions through the front every 7-14 days to prevent exhaustion and psychological collapse. This spread the burden across the entire army but ensured nearly every French soldier experienced Verdun.

**Tertiary Goal: Counteroffensive When Possible**

Under Robert Nivelle (who replaced Pétain in May 1916), French strategy shifted to aggressive counterattacks. By October, French forces had accumulated sufficient resources to launch a major counteroffensive, recapturing Douaumont on October 24 and Vaux on November 2.

### French Execution

French defense relied on artillery coordination, logistical endurance, and human willpower. La Voie Sacrée—the 75-kilometer road from Bar-le-Duc to Verdun—became the lifeline. At peak capacity, 3,900 trucks moved along this road, delivering 190,000 tons of supplies per week. One vehicle passed every 14 seconds. Maintenance crews worked continuously to repair shell damage and erosion.

French artillery, initially outnumbered, achieved parity by April 1916 through centralized command and constant reinforcement. By October, French guns outnumbered German artillery.

The French counteroffensive in October-December 1916 achieved what Germany had failed to do: rapid territorial gains using coordinated artillery and infantry tactics (the "rolling barrage"). By December 18, French forces had reclaimed most lost ground.

### Outcome

**German Failure**: Verdun did not break French morale. German casualties were nearly equal to French losses. The operation consumed resources Germany could not replace.

**French Victory**: Verdun was held. French strategic and symbolic objectives were achieved. But the cost—377,000 French casualties—contributed to the 1917 mutinies and long-term national trauma.

**Mutual Exhaustion**: Both armies emerged diminished. Verdun epitomized the futility of attrition warfare.

## CHAPTER 4: ARTILLERY DOCTRINE AND SCALE IN 1916

### The Dominance of Artillery

By 1916, artillery had become the dominant weapon of the Western Front. Estimates suggest 60-75% of all casualties were caused by artillery fire, not rifles or machine guns. Verdun confirmed this statistic. Artillery killed more men, destroyed more positions, and shaped tactics more than any other weapon system.

### German Artillery at Verdun

**Numbers and Concentration**

On February 21, 1916, Germany deployed approximately 1,220 guns along a 20-kilometer front:

- 420mm "Big Bertha" siege howitzers: 2 (used against Fort Douaumont)
- 305mm Austrian Škoda mortars: 13
- 210mm Mörser: 152
- 150mm heavy field howitzers: 306
- 77mm field guns: ~750

This concentration—one gun every 16 meters—was unprecedented. The opening bombardment delivered approximately one million shells in nine hours.

**Doctrine: Trommelfeuer (Drumfire)**

German artillery doctrine emphasized saturation bombardment: continuous fire that overlapped in time and space, creating an unbroken curtain of explosions. The goal was physical destruction of defenses and psychological destruction of defenders.

Trommelfeuer was not random. German planners divided the target area into grids, assigned batteries to each grid, and timed fire sequences to maintain constant pressure. Observers adjusted fire based on results.

**Shell Types**

German forces used multiple shell types tailored to specific targets:

- **High Explosive (HE)**: Standard shell for destroying trenches, dugouts, and obstacles.
- **Shrapnel**: Airburst shells scattering lead balls, effective against infantry in the open.
- **Gas Shells**: Phosgene (Green Cross) and diphosgene caused pulmonary edema and asphyxiation. Arsenic irritants (Blue Cross) forced removal of gas masks, making troops vulnerable to lethal gas.

### French Artillery at Verdun

**Initial Inferiority**

On February 21, 1916, French forces in the Verdun sector had approximately 270 guns—less than one-quarter the German total. Initial French responses were limited by lack of ammunition, inadequate positioning, and severed communications.

**Pétain's Reorganization**

Philippe Pétain, appointed February 26, immediately reorganized French artillery:

- **Centralized Command**: All artillery placed under a single commander (initially Robert Nivelle). This allowed coordinated fire missions across the entire sector.
- **Reinforcement**: Additional batteries rushed to Verdun via La Voie Sacrée. By April, French artillery approached parity with German guns.
- **Counter-Battery Focus**: French artillery prioritized destroying German guns rather than infantry targets. Observation aircraft spotted German batteries; French guns engaged them systematically.

By mid-1916, French artillery outnumbered German guns in some sectors. The balance shifted.

**The 75mm Canon de 75 modèle 1897 ("Soixante-Quinze")**

The French 75mm field gun was a technological marvel. Its hydro-pneumatic recoil system allowed rapid fire without re-aiming (15-20 rounds per minute sustained). At Verdun, over 1,000 French 75s provided continuous fire support.

**Nivelle's Innovation: The Rolling Barrage (Barrage Roulant)**

Robert Nivelle perfected the creeping or rolling barrage: artillery fire advancing in timed increments ahead of infantry assault. Shells landed 50-100 meters ahead of attacking troops, suppressing defenders and allowing rapid advance. This technique, demonstrated in the October 1916 French counteroffensive, became standard doctrine for the remainder of the war.

### Ammunition Expenditure

The scale of artillery fire at Verdun is difficult to comprehend:

**German Expenditure**:
- February 21, 1916: ~1,000,000 shells in 9 hours
- February-December 1916: Estimated 22,000,000 shells total

**French Expenditure**:
- February-December 1916: Estimated 15,000,000-18,000,000 shells

**Combined Total**: 37,000,000 to 40,000,000 shells over 303 days—an average of 120,000-130,000 shells per day across a front of approximately 20-30 kilometers.

For context: At peak intensity, Verdun experienced approximately one shell explosion every 10 seconds for hours or days at a time.

### Effects of Artillery

**Physical Destruction**

- Trenches collapsed and filled with debris.
- Forests were obliterated. Bois des Caures, Bois d'Haumont, and other wooded areas were reduced to splinters.
- Villages disappeared. Fleury, Douaumont, Vaux, and others were erased—literally ground into dust.
- Terrain became impassable. Shell craters overlapped, creating a moonscape of mud and water.

**Human Casualties**

Artillery caused 70-75% of Verdun casualties through:
- Direct hits (instant death)
- Shrapnel wounds (lacerations, infections)
- Burial alive (trench collapses)
- Concussion trauma (shell shock, ruptured organs)
- Gas inhalation (phosgene causing pulmonary edema)

**Psychological Impact**

Artillery created constant terror. Soldiers could not see the enemy, could not fight back, could not predict where shells would land. Survival was random. This randomness—the inability to control one's fate—caused widespread psychological trauma (shell shock, dissociation, fatalism).

### Artillery as the Game's Primary Threat

In *Only The Dead*, artillery is the primary threat because that was the historical reality. The game's shell recognition system, cover mechanics, and random death are not design choices meant to frustrate players—they are accurate simulations of what soldiers experienced.

Understanding artillery is essential to survival in the game, just as it was essential to survival at Verdun.

## CHAPTER 5: ROLE OF FORTS AND DEFENSIVE SYSTEMS

### The Séré de Rivières System

After the Franco-Prussian War (1870-1871), France constructed a network of fortresses along its eastern border to deter future German invasions. This system, designed by General Raymond Adolphe Séré de Rivières, included the Verdun fortress complex: a ring of interconnected forts designed for mutual support.

Key forts included:
- Fort Douaumont (largest)
- Fort Vaux
- Fort Souville
- Fort Tavannes
- Fort Belleville
- Fort Moulainville

Each fort was a polygonal concrete and earth structure with underground galleries, armored observation posts, and turret-mounted artillery.

### The 1915 Disarmament

In 1915, French High Command believed modern siege artillery had made fortresses obsolete. The rapid fall of Belgian forts (Liège, Namur) to German 420mm howitzers in 1914 seemed to confirm this judgment.

Consequently, heavy guns were removed from Verdun's forts and redeployed elsewhere. Garrisons were reduced to skeleton crews. Fort Douaumont, designed to hold 500-800 men, was defended by 56 territorial artillerists (elderly reservists) on February 25, 1916.

This decision proved disastrous.

### The Fall of Fort Douaumont (February 25, 1916)

Fort Douaumont was the strongest position in the Verdun complex: concrete walls 2.5 meters thick, galleries 30 meters underground, and commanding views of the surrounding terrain.

On February 25, 1916, a small detachment of German infantry from the 24th Brandenburg Regiment approached the fort during a snowstorm. Finding it virtually undefended, they entered through an unguarded embrasure. After brief confused fighting in the dark galleries, the 56 French defenders surrendered.

Germany announced the capture as a great victory. France was shocked. The loss of Douaumont became a national humiliation and a rallying cry: the fort must be recaptured.

### The Siege of Fort Vaux (June 1-7, 1916)

Fort Vaux, smaller than Douaumont, was garrisoned by approximately 600 men under Major Sylvain-Eugène Raynal when the German assault began on June 1, 1916.

**The Siege**:
- German artillery bombarded the fort with 305mm shells for five days, destroying surface structures and sealing exits.
- German infantry entered the fort's galleries on June 2, leading to hand-to-hand combat in pitch darkness.
- Flamethrowers were used in the narrow tunnels, creating horrific conditions.
- The fort's water cistern was destroyed early in the siege. Men had approximately 2 liters of water per person over seven days total—not per day, but total. Wounded men died of thirst. Some drank their own urine. Others licked moisture from the walls.

On June 4, Raynal sent a final message via carrier pigeon (the bird, named "Vaillant," successfully reached French lines despite being wounded). The message: "We are holding, but under very severe gas attack. Urgent relief required."

Relief did not come. On June 7, with water exhausted and men delirious, Raynal surrendered. German forces granted him honors for his defense. Vaux fell, but the prolonged resistance demonstrated that forts, properly defended, were not obsolete.

### The Recapture (October 24 - November 2, 1916)

By October 1916, German forces at Verdun were exhausted and depleted by the Battle of the Somme. French forces, reinforced and reorganized, launched a counteroffensive.

**Fort Douaumont Recaptured**: October 24, 1916
Moroccan colonial troops (Régiment d'Infanterie Coloniale du Maroc) and the French 38th Division attacked under cover of a rolling barrage. German defenders, demoralized and outnumbered, abandoned the fort. By evening, French forces reoccupied Douaumont. Six thousand German prisoners were taken.

**Fort Vaux Evacuated**: November 1-2, 1916
German forces evacuated Vaux during the night, recognizing the position was untenable. French forces reoccupied the fort on November 2 without resistance.

### Lessons Learned

The experience at Verdun reversed the pre-war consensus on fortifications:

- Forts properly garrisoned and supplied could withstand modern artillery.
- Deep concrete structures protected men from bombardment better than field trenches.
- Forts had psychological value beyond their tactical utility.

These lessons influenced French post-war fortification planning, culminating in the construction of the Maginot Line in the 1930s—a system designed to avoid another Verdun by making defensive positions impregnable.

## CHAPTER 6: WEATHER AND TERRAIN CONDITIONS

### Geographic Context

Verdun sits in northeastern France, approximately 250 kilometers east of Paris. The Meuse River runs through the city. The battlefield extends across both banks:

- **Right Bank (East)**: Includes Fort Douaumont, Fort Vaux, and the villages of Fleury and Vaux.
- **Left Bank (West)**: Includes Mort-Homme, Hill 304, and Cumières.

Elevation ranges from 165 meters (Meuse River valley) to 390 meters (Fort Vaux). The terrain consists of rolling hills, ridges, and formerly forested areas.

### Pre-War Terrain

Before February 1916, the Verdun region was farmland and forest. Villages housed civilian populations. Roads connected settlements. The landscape was ordinary rural France.

### Post-Battle Terrain Transformation

By December 1916, the battlefield was unrecognizable. Artillery bombardment had transformed the terrain into a lunar landscape:

- **Forests Obliterated**: Bois des Caures, Herbebois, Bois d'Haumont, and others were reduced to broken stumps. Photographs show forests transformed into fields of shattered wood splinters.
- **Villages Erased**: Fleury, Douaumont, Vaux, Beaumont, Ornes, Louvemont, Cumières, Haumont, and Bezonvaux were ground into rubble and dust. Today, these villages remain legally "Mort pour la France"—died for France. They are uninhabited memorials.
- **Craters Everywhere**: Shell craters overlapped to create impassable terrain. Some craters were 10-15 meters wide and 3-5 meters deep. Water collected in craters, creating toxic pools.
- **Soil Contaminated**: Explosives, gas residue, human remains, and metal fragments mixed into the soil. The Zone Rouge remains restricted due to contamination and unexploded ordnance.

### Seasonal Weather Conditions

The battle lasted ten months, spanning winter, spring, summer, and autumn. Each season brought different challenges.

**Winter (February - March 1916)**

- Temperatures: -5°C to 5°C (daytime)
- Snow and sleet common
- Frozen ground initially aided movement (mud was solid)
- Cold caused frostbite, hypothermia, and increased caloric needs
- Trench life was miserable but terrain was passable

**Spring (April - May 1916)**

- Temperatures: 5°C to 15°C
- Heavy rain (April-May are wet months in northeastern France)
- Ground thawed, creating deep mud
- Trenches flooded
- Movement became exhausting
- Trench foot epidemic began
- Wounded drowned in shell craters filled with water and mud

**Summer (June - August 1916)**

- Temperatures: 15°C to 30°C
- Heat created different problems:
  - Decomposing corpses (thousands unburied) created unbearable stench
  - Flies swarmed (disease vectors)
  - Thirst became critical (water scarce)
  - Dysentery spread rapidly
  - Soldiers in Fort Vaux during the June siege suffered acute thirst in summer heat

**Autumn (September - November 1916)**

- Temperatures: 5°C to 15°C
- Rain returned (October-November wet)
- Mud returned
- Preparation for winter (supply of warm clothing, firewood)
- French counteroffensive launched October 24 in cold, wet conditions

**Winter Returns (December 1916)**

- Temperatures: -5°C to 5°C
- Snow and freezing rain
- Battle concluded December 18 with both sides exhausted and the ground frozen

### The Mud

Mud at Verdun was not ordinary mud. Artillery pulverized the soil into fine powder. Rain mixed this powder into a thick, bottomless slurry. This mud had unique characteristics:

- **Depth**: Trench floors were commonly 10-50 cm deep in mud. Shell craters filled with 1-2 meters of liquid mud. Men who fell into craters drowned.
- **Consistency**: Thick, clinging, and nearly impossible to escape once trapped. Soldiers described it as "alive."
- **Effects on Movement**: Walking in mud was exhausting. Running was nearly impossible. Equipment bogged down. Vehicles became stuck. Supply transport slowed.
- **Health Consequences**: Constant wetness caused trench foot. Infections spread. Exhaustion from moving in mud weakened soldiers.

Testimony from Maurice Genevoix: "The mud is the enemy. Not the Germans. The mud. It holds you, pulls you down, drowns you. We lost men to the mud. They fell into a crater and couldn't climb out. We heard them screaming, but by the time we got ropes, the mud had taken them."

### The Zone Rouge (Red Zone)

After the war, the French government designated approximately 100 square kilometers around Verdun as the Zone Rouge—an area so contaminated and dangerous that human habitation was prohibited.

**Contamination Sources**:
- Unexploded ordnance (shells, grenades, mines)
- Toxic chemicals (phosgene, mustard gas residue, heavy metals)
- Human remains (an estimated 100,000+ bodies were never recovered)

**Current Status**:
- Restricted access (permits required)
- French Démineurs (bomb disposal experts) continue to remove ordnance—approximately 40 tons per year, over a century later
- Agricultural use prohibited
- Forestry allowed in limited areas (trees grow, but soil remains dangerous)

### Terrain in *Only The Dead*

The game's terrain is based on real elevation data, crater density estimates, and photographic evidence. Movement difficulty, visibility limits, and environmental hazards reflect historical conditions. The mud is not an aesthetic choice—it is a documented reality.

## CHAPTER 7: HUMAN AND MATERIAL COST

### Casualty Statistics

The Battle of Verdun cost approximately 714,000 casualties over 303 days—an average of 2,356 casualties per day for ten months.

**French Casualties**: ~377,231 total
- Killed or missing: ~163,000
- Wounded: ~214,000

**German Casualties**: ~337,000 total
- Killed or missing: ~143,000
- Wounded: ~194,000

These numbers are estimates. Record-keeping during the battle was incomplete. Some bodies were never recovered. Some men listed as "missing" were buried by shell explosions or drowned in mud. Exact figures will never be known.

### Casualty Breakdown by Cause

Artillery caused 70-75% of casualties at Verdun. The remaining 25-30% were caused by:
- Small arms fire (rifles, machine guns): 15-20%
- Gas: 3-5%
- Other (accidents, disease, exposure): 5-10%

### Medical Realities

**Evacuation Timeline**:
1. **Front-Line Aid (Poste de Secours)**: Immediate first aid, triage
2. **Regimental Aid Station**: Basic surgery, tetanus antitoxin
3. **Field Hospital (Ambulance)**: Emergency surgery, stabilization
4. **Evacuation Hospital**: Specialist care
5. **Base Hospital**: Long-term recovery

Survival depended on speed. Men who reached a field hospital within 6 hours had a 60-70% survival rate. Delayed evacuation drastically reduced survival.

**Common Injuries**:
- **Shrapnel Wounds**: Metal fragments causing lacerations, infections
- **Amputations**: Gas gangrene or destroyed limbs required immediate amputation. Mortality: 20-40% depending on location.
- **Head Wounds**: 50-70% mortality due to infection and limited surgical capability
- **Abdominal Wounds**: 80-90% mortality (peritonitis was almost always fatal without antibiotics)
- **Gas Wounds**: Phosgene caused pulmonary edema. Heavy exposure: 80-90% fatal. No effective treatment existed in 1916.
- **Burns**: Flamethrowers and explosions caused severe burns. Over 40% body surface area: usually fatal.

**Shell Shock**: Thousands of cases. Symptoms included tremors, paralysis, mutism, nightmares, and dissociation. Treatment was primitive. Many men were returned to the front despite incomplete recovery.

### Logistical Scale

**La Voie Sacrée** (The Sacred Way): The 75-kilometer road from Bar-le-Duc to Verdun was France's lifeline.

- **Trucks**: 3,900 at peak capacity
- **Frequency**: One vehicle every 14 seconds
- **Tonnage**: 190,000 tons of supplies per week
- **Maintenance**: 8,500 territorial soldiers repaired the road continuously
- **Duration**: Maintained for ten months under constant pressure

Without La Voie Sacrée, Verdun would have fallen.

**Ammunition Consumption**:
- French 75mm shells: Millions
- Heavy shells (155mm, 210mm, 305mm): Millions
- Total shells fired (both sides): 37-40 million

**Food and Water**:
- Daily ration requirement for 300,000+ men in the Verdun sector: ~500 tons of food per day
- Water: 300,000+ liters per day
- Wine: 75,000 liters per day (official French ration)

### Psychological Cost

**The French Mutinies of 1917**: One year after Verdun, the French Army experienced widespread mutinies. Approximately 40,000 soldiers refused orders to attack. The mutinies were not caused solely by Verdun, but the battle's trauma was a significant factor. Soldiers had endured too much.

**National Trauma**: Verdun became synonymous with futility, suffering, and endurance in French collective memory. Nearly every French family lost someone at Verdun or knew someone who served there. The psychological impact persisted for generations.

**German Disillusionment**: For Germany, Verdun was a costly failure. Crown Prince Wilhelm's reputation was damaged. Falkenhayn was replaced. The operation achieved none of its strategic goals.

### The Ossuary of Douaumont

After the war, approximately 130,000 unidentified human remains were collected from the battlefield and interred in the Douaumont Ossuary, completed in 1932. Viewing windows allow visitors to see piles of bones—femurs, skulls, ribs—stacked in the crypt.

The ossuary stands as a memorial to the unidentified dead. Its inscription: "Silence. Here lies the glory and the sorrow of Verdun."

### Material Cost Beyond Human Life

- **Villages Destroyed**: 9 never rebuilt
- **Forests Destroyed**: Thousands of hectares
- **Soil Poisoned**: Zone Rouge remains dangerous over a century later
- **Infrastructure Destroyed**: Roads, bridges, farms, churches
- **Economic Cost**: Incalculable. France allocated enormous resources to Verdun, resources that could not be used elsewhere.

### Why This Matters for the Game

*Only The Dead* does not shy away from these costs. The numbers above are not abstract. They represent decisions you will make: whether to risk evacuation, whether to stay in a collapsing dugout, whether to advance under bombardment. The game asks you to experience a fraction of what those 714,000 casualties endured.

This is not entertainment. This is education through simulation.

### Artillery Range Circles

Understanding artillery range is critical for comprehending the battlefield's lethality. Every point within range of enemy guns was potentially under fire.

**German Artillery Positions (February 1916)**:
- Located 8-15 kilometers behind German front lines
- Heavy guns (305mm, 420mm): Range 9-12 kilometers
  - **Coverage**: Could hit French positions 3-4 kilometers behind front lines
- Medium howitzers (150mm, 210mm): Range 8-11 kilometers
  - **Coverage**: Could hit French front lines and support trenches
- Field guns (77mm): Range 9 kilometers
  - **Coverage**: Direct and indirect fire on front-line trenches

**French Artillery Positions**:
- Initially positioned 5-10 kilometers behind lines
- 75mm field guns: Range 8.5 kilometers
- 155mm howitzers: Range 16 kilometers (could reach German artillery positions)

**Implication**: Nearly the entire battlefield was within artillery range of both sides. There was no "safe zone" in the forward areas.

### No-Man's-Land

No-Man's-Land—the space between French and German front lines—varied by sector:

- **Narrowest Point**: ~50 meters (some sectors)
- **Widest Point**: ~400 meters (other sectors)
- **Average**: 150-250 meters

Crossing No-Man's-Land under fire was suicidal during daytime. Night patrols used darkness for cover but faced the same cratered, muddy, corpse-strewn terrain.

- **Crater Density**: 5-10 craters per 100 square meters in the worst areas
- **Crater Dimensions**:

  - 155mm shell: 3-5 meters wide, 1.5-2 meters deep
  - 305mm shell: 8-10 meters wide, 3-5 meters deep
  - 420mm shell: 12-15 meters wide, 5-8 meters deep

## CHAPTER 2: FORT DOUAUMONT AND FORT VAUX STRUCTURAL DIAGRAMS

### Fort Douaumont: Structure and Layout

Fort Douaumont was the largest fortress in the Verdun defensive system.

**Dimensions**:
- **Length**: Approximately 400 meters
- **Width**: Approximately 300 meters
- **Total Area**: ~12 hectares (including glacis and outer defensive works)

**Construction**:
- **Walls**: Reinforced concrete 2.5 meters thick
- **Roof**: Concrete with earth covering, total thickness 3-4 meters
- **Galleries**: Underground tunnels and chambers extending 20-30 meters below surface
- **Design**: Polygonal trace (irregular pentagon shape) with bastions at corners

**Defensive Features**:
- **Armored Turrets** (Pre-1915 disarmament):
  - Two 155mm gun turrets (removed 1915)
  - Two 75mm gun turrets (removed 1915)
  - Machine gun turrets (some retained)
- **Observation Posts**: Armored cupolas with periscopes
- **Firing Embrasures**: Slots in walls for rifle and machine gun fire
- **Moat**: Dry moat 8-10 meters deep surrounding fort (could be swept by defensive fire)

**Interior Layout**:
- **Galleries**: Network of underground corridors connecting chambers
- **Barracks**: Rooms designed to house 500-800 men (bunks, tables, stoves)
- **Ammunition Storage**: Reinforced chambers for shells and explosives
- **Water Cistern**: Underground reservoir (capacity ~100,000 liters)
- **Kitchen and Latrines**: Basic facilities for garrison
- **Command Post**: Central chamber for officers
- **Medical Station**: Small aid post with surgical capability

**Garrison on February 25, 1916**:
- 56 territorial artillerists (elderly reservists, not combat infantry)
- No heavy weapons (turrets removed)
- Minimal supplies

**Why It Fell**:
- Inadequate garrison
- No heavy weapons
- Surprise approach during snowstorm
- Lack of coordinated defense

**Interior Conditions During German Occupation (Feb-Oct 1916)**:
- Overcrowded (hundreds of German soldiers sheltered during bombardments)
- Poor ventilation (smoke, gas, foul air)
- Disease (dysentery, respiratory infections)
- French artillery constantly bombarded the fort trying to destroy it or make it untenable

### Fort Vaux: Structure and Layout

Fort Vaux was smaller than Douaumont but more modern in some respects.

**Dimensions**:
- **Length**: Approximately 300 meters
- **Width**: Approximately 200 meters
- **Total Area**: ~6 hectares

**Construction**:
- **Walls**: Reinforced concrete 2 meters thick
- **Galleries**: Underground chambers 15-25 meters deep
- **Design**: Similar polygonal trace but more compact

**Defensive Features**:
- **Armored Turrets**: One 75mm gun turret (retained in 1916)
- **Machine Gun Posts**: Multiple positions covering approaches
- **Observation Cupola**: Armored observation post

**Interior Layout**:
- **Galleries**: Narrow tunnels (1.5 meters wide) connecting chambers
- **Barracks**: Smaller capacity than Douaumont (designed for 200-300 men)
- **Water Cistern**: Located in lower gallery—critically, this was destroyed early in June siege
- **Ventilation**: Limited; smoke and gas accumulated quickly
- **Command Post**: Central chamber

**Garrison During Siege (June 1-7, 1916)**:
- ~600 men (including wounded from nearby trenches)
- Commander: Major Sylvain-Eugène Raynal
- Supplies: Limited ammunition, no water after cistern destroyed

**The Siege: Day-by-Day Conditions**

**June 1**: German bombardment begins. Surface structures destroyed. Exits partially blocked.

**June 2**: German infantry enter galleries. Hand-to-hand combat in darkness. Flamethrowers used in tunnels (temperature exceeds 50°C, men suffocate from fumes).

**June 3-6**: Water exhausted. Men drink condensation from walls, urine, or nothing. Wounded die of thirst faster than wounds. Air quality deteriorates (smoke, gas, cordite fumes). Communications severed.

**June 4**: Carrier pigeon "Vaillant" sent with message. Bird wounded but reaches French lines. Message: "We are holding, but under very severe gas attack. Urgent relief required."

**June 7**: Raynal surrenders. Men delirious from thirst. German officers provide water immediately and grant honors for defense.

**Interior Atmosphere**:
- Darkness (lamps consumed oxygen)
- Deafening explosions overhead (constant vibration)
- Smell of death (wounded, excrement, gas)
- Heat (summer, no ventilation, bodies packed together)
- Thirst (dominant sensation—men hallucinated about water)

### Comparative Analysis: Douaumont vs. Vaux

| Feature | Fort Douaumont | Fort Vaux |
|---------|----------------|-----------|
| **Size** | Larger (~12 hectares) | Smaller (~6 hectares) |
| **Garrison Capacity** | 500-800 men | 200-300 men |
| **Capture Date** | Feb 25, 1916 (minimal resistance) | June 7, 1916 (after 7-day siege) |
| **Recapture Date** | Oct 24, 1916 (assault) | Nov 2, 1916 (evacuated by Germans) |
| **Symbolic Importance** | Highest (largest fort) | High (epic defense) |
| **Condition Post-War** | Heavily damaged but intact | Heavily damaged but intact |
| **Current Status** | Museum (visitors can tour) | Museum (visitors can tour) |

### Why Forts Mattered

Despite the 1915 belief that forts were obsolete, Verdun demonstrated their value:

**Psychological**: Forts were symbols. Their loss or retention affected morale.

**Tactical**: Forts provided observation points over surrounding terrain. Holding forts meant controlling key ground.

**Protective**: Deep concrete galleries protected men from bombardment better than field trenches.

**Logistical**: Forts housed command posts, medical stations, and supply depots.

The lesson learned: Forts remain valuable if properly garrisoned, supplied, and integrated into defensive systems. This informed post-war fortification design (e.g., Maginot Line).

## CHAPTER 3: TRENCH CONSTRUCTION AND DUGOUT CROSS-SECTIONS

### Standard French Trench (1916 Specifications)

French trenches at Verdun followed doctrinal guidelines but were often improvised or deteriorated by shelling.

**Trench Dimensions**:
- **Width**: 1.0-1.5 meters at floor level
- **Depth**: 2.0-2.5 meters from floor to top of parapet
- **Length**: Trenches extended kilometers (zigzag pattern to limit enfilade fire)

**Trench Profile (Cross-Section)**:

```
[Text-Based Diagram]

                        PARAPET (sandbags)
                    /‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾\
                   /                          \
 Ground Level: ===================================== (No-Man's-Land beyond)
                   |                          |
                   |  FIRE STEP (0.5m high)  |
                   |  [wooden platform]      |
       1.5-2.0m    |                          |
        deep       |                          |
                   |                          |
                   |  TRENCH FLOOR           |
                   |  [duckboards over mud]  |
                   ============================

       Width: 1.0-1.5 meters

PARADOS (rear wall): Similar height to parapet but lower (1-1.5m)
```

**Components**:

**1. Parapet (Front Wall)**:
- Height: 1.5-2.0 meters above ground level
- Construction: Sandbags filled with earth, stacked in offset rows
- Purpose: Protection from enemy rifle and machine gun fire
- Maintenance: Constant (sandbags deteriorated, required daily repair)

**2. Fire Step**:
- Height: 0.5 meters above trench floor
- Construction: Wooden planks supported by stakes
- Purpose: Soldiers stood on fire step to shoot over parapet during attacks or sentry duty
- Usage: Crouch down behind parapet when not firing

**3. Trench Floor**:
- Surface: Duckboards (wooden slats) laid over mud
- Reality: Duckboards often broken, submerged, or missing
- Mud Depth: 10-50 cm common (ankle to knee-deep)
- Drainage: Attempted via sumps (low points with pumps or bailing), but largely ineffective

**4. Parados (Rear Wall)**:
- Height: 1.0-1.5 meters
- Purpose: Protection from shells landing behind trench (rear approach)
- Construction: Similar to parapet but lower

**5. Traverse**:
- Every 10-20 meters, trenches included "traverses"—sharp 90° turns or zigzags
- Purpose: Limit enfilade fire (enemy shooting down length of trench) and limit blast radius if shell landed inside trench

### Dugout Types: Shallow vs. Deep

**Shallow Dugout (Abri Léger)**:

```
[Text-Based Diagram - Side View]

Ground Level: ===================================
                     |
                     |  Entrance
                     V
              _______________
             /               \  <-- Roof: sandbags + wood beams
            /                 \
   Trench   |  DUGOUT INTERIOR  |  <-- 1-2 meters deep into trench wall
   Wall --> |  [2-4 men]        |
            |                   |
            |___________________|

        Depth into wall: 2-4 meters
        Height: 1.5-2.0 meters (crouch or sit)
```

**Construction**:
- Excavated horizontally into trench wall
- Supported by wooden beams (roof and sides)
- Roof covered with sandbags for protection
- Entrance usually just an opening (sometimes curtain for gas protection)

**Capacity**: 2-4 men

**Protection**:
- Rifle fire: Complete
- Shrapnel: Good
- 75mm shells: Moderate (roof may collapse if close hit)
- 155mm+ shells: Poor (likely collapses)
- Gas: Partial (gas sinks, dugout fills unless sealed)

**Living Conditions**:
- Dark (candles or small oil lamps only)
- Damp (water seeps through walls)
- Cold in winter (~10°C inside), warmer than outside
- Cramped (sit hunched or lie down)
- Stuffy (poor ventilation, smoke from lamps)

**Deep Dugout (Abri Profond)**:

```
[Text-Based Diagram - Side View]

Ground Level: ===================================
                     |
                     | Entrance (stairs or ladder)
                     V
                    [ ]  <-- Entry shaft
                    [ ]
                    [ ]  <-- 3-6 meters deep
                    [ ]
              _______[ ]_______
             /                 \  <-- Vertical shaft
            /  MAIN CHAMBER    \
           |   [10-30 men]      |  <-- Underground room
           |                    |
           |  [bunks, tables]   |
           |____________________|

        Depth: 3-6 meters underground
        Size: 20-40 square meters (varies)
```

**Construction**:
- Vertical shaft (stairs or ladder) descending 3-6 meters
- Main chamber excavated at bottom
- Reinforced with heavy timber or concrete supports
- Ventilation shaft (if engineered)
- Multiple entrances sometimes (escape route if one collapses)

**Capacity**: 10-30 men

**Protection**:
- Rifle fire: Complete
- Shrapnel: Complete
- Light/medium shells (75mm-155mm): Complete protection
- Heavy shells (210mm-305mm): Very good (survives most hits unless direct)
- Gas: Good (gas takes time to sink; entrance can be sealed)

**Living Conditions**:
- Very dark (lamps essential, consume oxygen)
- Damp (constant water seepage, puddles on floor)
- Warmer in winter (earth insulation, 10-12°C year-round)
- Less cramped (can stand upright in some)
- Better air if ventilation shaft present
- Bunks if constructed (wooden platforms for sleeping)

**Danger**: Burial alive if entrance collapses from nearby shell hit. Garrison trapped underground with limited air.

### Communication Trenches

**Purpose**: Connect front-line, support, and reserve trenches. Used for movement, resupply, and evacuation.

**Design**:
- Zigzag pattern (same reason as front trenches—limit enfilade fire)
- Depth: Usually shallower than front-line trenches (1.5-2.0 meters)
- Width: Slightly wider to allow stretcher-bearers to pass

**Reality**: Communication trenches were constantly damaged by artillery. Movement along them was slow, dangerous, and exhausting (mud, shell damage, corpses).

### Barbed Wire Obstacles

**Placement**: 10-30 meters in front of trenches

**Construction**:
- Wooden or iron stakes driven into ground
- Barbed wire strung between stakes in multiple layers
- Depth: 5-10 meters of wire obstacles

**Purpose**: Slow enemy infantry during assault, making them vulnerable to defensive fire

**Reality**: Artillery bombardment often cut wire, but not completely. Attackers still had to navigate wire while under fire. At night, barbed wire was nearly invisible—patrols became entangled and exposed.

### Sanitation: Latrines

**Location**: Rear of trench, downwind if possible

**Construction**:
- Pit dug 1-2 meters deep
- Wooden plank with hole over pit
- Sometimes covered with tarp or corrugated iron

**Conditions**:
- Overflowing frequently (not emptied often enough)
- Stench unbearable
- Flies everywhere (disease vector)
- Exposed to shelling (using latrine during bombardment was high-risk)
- No privacy

**Disease Risk**: High. Latrines were breeding grounds for dysentery, cholera, and other diseases.

### Material Requirements for 100 Meters of Trench

To construct and maintain 100 meters of standard trench:

- **Sandbags**: 10,000-15,000 (filled with earth)
- **Wooden beams**: 50-100 (for dugout roofs, fire steps, supports)
- **Duckboards**: 200-300 wooden slats
- **Barbed wire**: 500-1,000 meters
- **Stakes**: 200-300 (for wire obstacles)
- **Labor**: 50-100 men working for 2-3 days (initial construction)
- **Maintenance**: 5-10 men per day (ongoing repairs)

**Reality at Verdun**: Trench systems were constantly destroyed by artillery. Entire sectors would be obliterated, requiring complete reconstruction. Maintenance crews worked under fire, often at night.

## CHAPTER 4: SHELL CRATER DENSITY AND BLAST PATTERN EXAMPLES

### Understanding Crater Formation

When an artillery shell impacts earth, it creates a crater through explosive force. The size and depth of the crater depend on shell caliber, shell type, angle of impact, and soil composition.

### Crater Dimensions by Shell Caliber

**75mm Field Gun Shell**:
- Crater Width: 1-2 meters
- Crater Depth: 0.5-1 meter
- Lethal Radius: 10 meters (shrapnel and blast)
- Dangerous Radius: 20 meters (debris, concussion)

**155mm Howitzer Shell**:
- Crater Width: 3-5 meters
- Crater Depth: 1.5-2.5 meters
- Lethal Radius: 20-30 meters
- Dangerous Radius: 50 meters

**210mm Mortar Shell**:
- Crater Width: 5-8 meters
- Crater Depth: 2-3 meters
- Lethal Radius: 30-40 meters
- Dangerous Radius: 80 meters

**305mm Siege Howitzer Shell**:
- Crater Width: 8-10 meters
- Crater Depth: 3-5 meters
- Lethal Radius: 40-50 meters
- Dangerous Radius: 100+ meters

**420mm "Big Bertha" Shell**:
- Crater Width: 12-15 meters
- Crater Depth: 5-8 meters
- Lethal Radius: 50-100 meters
- Dangerous Radius: 150+ meters

### Crater Density in Different Sectors

**Lightly Shelled Areas** (Reserve trenches, rear areas):
- Crater Density: 1-2 craters per 100 square meters
- Terrain: Damaged but passable
- Vegetation: Partially intact

**Moderately Shelled Areas** (Support trenches, contested ground):
- Crater Density: 3-5 craters per 100 square meters
- Terrain: Heavily cratered, difficult movement
- Vegetation: Mostly destroyed

**Heavily Shelled Areas** (Front-line trenches, key positions like Fleury, Douaumont, Mort-Homme):
- Crater Density: 5-10+ craters per 100 square meters
- Terrain: Craters overlapping, creating continuous disruption
- Vegetation: Completely obliterated
- Soil: Pulverized into fine powder (mud when wet)

**Photographic Evidence**: Aerial photographs from 1916 show sectors where individual craters cannot be distinguished—the entire surface is uniformly cratered.

### Blast Effects Beyond Craters

**Concussion**:
- Even if shrapnel misses you, the blast wave can rupture internal organs (lungs, intestines)
- Temporary or permanent deafness from pressure wave
- Knocked unconscious from concussion
- Eardrums ruptured

**Burial**:
- Trenches collapsed by nearby shell hits, burying men alive
- Shell craters created earth displacement—tons of earth thrown into the air, landing on trenches and men
- Men buried in mud died from suffocation, not direct wounds

**Fragmentation**:
- Shrapnel (metal shell casing fragments) travels 100-200 meters at lethal velocity
- Secondary fragmentation (rocks, wood, equipment) propelled by blast
- Wounds from fragments: lacerations, embedded metal, infections

### Shell Crater Water Accumulation

**Water Collection**: Shell craters filled with rainwater, groundwater, and runoff from trenches.

**Water Quality**: Toxic mixture—
- Chemicals from explosives (TNT residue)
- Human waste from latrines and bodies
- Heavy metals from shell fragments
- Bacteria from decomposition

**Depth**: Craters from heavy shells filled with 1-2 meters of water and liquid mud.

**Danger**: Men who fell into water-filled craters often drowned. Liquid mud at the bottom was thick enough to trap and pull men under. Wounded men without help could not escape.

**Testimony**: "I saw him fall into the crater. He tried to climb out but the mud held him. We threw ropes, but before we could pull him out, he sank. We could hear him screaming for maybe a minute. Then silence." — French soldier, April 1916

### Crater Navigation in *Only The Dead*

Players will encounter crater-filled terrain requiring careful navigation:
- **Crossing craters**: Slow movement, risk of falling
- **Water-filled craters**: Drowning risk if wounded or exhausted
- **Using craters for cover**: Temporary protection from fire, but exposed to subsequent shells

Understanding crater formation helps players assess terrain danger and plan movement accordingly.

- **Miscellaneous**: Hardtack, cheese, wine (~1 kg)

**TOTAL WEIGHT**: ~27-35 kg (varies by what is carried)

**When Wet**: Add 3-5 kg (uniform absorbs water, mud clings to boots and equipment)

**When Carrying Extra**: Soldiers sometimes carried extra ammunition, tools, or supplies for squad/section use, increasing weight to 40+ kg.

### Weight Impact on Movement and Stamina

**Dry Conditions, Flat Terrain**:
- Walking speed: ~4-5 km/hour
- Stamina consumption: Moderate
- Can march for hours (with rest breaks)

**Wet Conditions, Mud**:
- Walking speed: ~1-2 km/hour (slowed by mud sucking at boots)
- Stamina consumption: High
- Exhaustion within 1-2 hours without rest

**Climbing (into/out of trenches, craters)**:
- Stamina consumption: Very high
- Repeated climbing drains stamina quickly

**Carrying Wounded Comrade**:
- Additional weight: ~60-80 kg (person + equipment)
- Stamina consumption: Extreme
- Requires two men (stretcher) or one man dragging (very slow)

### What Soldiers Discarded to Reduce Weight

When exhausted, soldiers discarded non-essential items:

**Commonly Discarded**:
- Overcoat (kept only in winter)
- Extra ammunition beyond immediate need
- Extra rations
- Blanket
- Entrenching tool (if trenches already dug)

**Never Discarded**:
- Rifle (required; discarding rifle = court-martial)
- Gas mask (required; discarding = death in gas attack)
- Water bottle
- Immediate rations

### Comparative: German Infantry Loadout

German soldiers carried similar weight (~25-35 kg) with comparable equipment:
- Gewehr 98 rifle (~4.1 kg)
- 60-90 rounds ammunition (~1 kg)
- Tornister (backpack)
- M1916 Stahlhelm (~1.3 kg, heavier than French helmet)
- Gas mask
- Grenades (Stielhandgranate "potato masher" ~0.6 kg each)

### Weight in *Only The Dead*

The game simulates equipment weight affecting stamina and movement speed:
- Full load: Normal movement, moderate stamina drain
- Wet equipment: Slower movement, higher stamina drain
- Carrying wounded: Very slow movement, rapid stamina drain
- Discarding equipment: Faster movement but reduces inventory capacity and options

Players must balance what to carry versus stamina management—just as soldiers did.

## CHAPTER 6: ARTILLERY RANGE ARCS AND FIRING TABLES

### Understanding Artillery Range and Coverage

Artillery effectiveness depends on range, angle of fire, and shell type. Different guns served different roles.

### German Artillery: Range and Purpose

**77mm FK 16 (Feldkanone - Field Gun)**:
- **Range**: 9 km maximum
- **Rate of Fire**: 10-15 rounds per minute
- **Shell Weight**: 6.8 kg
- **Primary Use**: Direct fire on enemy trenches, anti-personnel, suppression
- **Trajectory**: Relatively flat (field gun, not howitzer)

**150mm sFH 13 (schwere Feldhaubitze - Heavy Field Howitzer)**:
- **Range**: 8.5 km maximum
- **Rate of Fire**: 4-5 rounds per minute
- **Shell Weight**: 40-43 kg
- **Primary Use**: Destruction of trenches, dugouts, wire obstacles
- **Trajectory**: High-angle plunging fire

**210mm Mörser (Mortar)**:
- **Range**: 11 km maximum
- **Rate of Fire**: 2 rounds per minute
- **Shell Weight**: 113 kg
- **Primary Use**: Heavy bombardment, destroying fortifications
- **Trajectory**: Very high angle

**305mm Škoda Howitzer (Austrian)**:
- **Range**: 12 km maximum
- **Rate of Fire**: 1 round per 5 minutes
- **Shell Weight**: 380 kg
- **Primary Use**: Siege warfare, fort destruction
- **Trajectory**: Extreme high angle

**420mm "Big Bertha" (Dicke Bertha)**:
- **Range**: 9.3 km maximum
- **Rate of Fire**: 1 round per 8-10 minutes
- **Shell Weight**: 820 kg
- **Primary Use**: Destroying reinforced concrete forts
- **Penetration**: 3-4 meters of reinforced concrete
- **Note**: Limited mobility, slow rate of fire, used sparingly

### French Artillery: Range and Purpose

**75mm Canon de 75 modèle 1897 ("Soixante-Quinze")**:
- **Range**: 8.5 km maximum
- **Rate of Fire**: 15-20 rounds per minute (rapid fire), 6-8 sustained
- **Shell Weight**: 5.3-7.25 kg (varies by shell type)
- **Primary Use**: Backbone of French artillery, all purposes (anti-personnel, suppression, barrage)
- **Trajectory**: Flat to moderate

**155mm GPF (Grande Puissance Filloux)**:
- **Range**: 16.2 km maximum
- **Rate of Fire**: 2 rounds per minute
- **Shell Weight**: 43 kg
- **Primary Use**: Counter-battery fire, heavy bombardment, destroying fortifications
- **Trajectory**: High angle

**220mm Canon Long**:
- **Range**: 22 km maximum
- **Shell Weight**: 108 kg
- **Primary Use**: Long-range counter-battery, reaching German artillery positions
- **Trajectory**: Moderate to high

**274mm Canon de 274 (Naval Gun)**:
- **Range**: 30 km maximum
- **Shell Weight**: 240 kg
- **Primary Use**: Ultra-long-range bombardment, strategic targets
- **Note**: Removed from ships, mounted on railway carriages, deployed late 1916

### Range Coverage Implications

**Overlapping Fire Zones**:

At Verdun, the front lines were narrow enough that artillery from both sides could reach deep into enemy territory:

- German 150mm howitzers could hit French positions 8.5 km behind the front line.
- French 155mm guns could reach German artillery positions 16 km behind German lines.
- This meant almost no area within 10-15 km of the front was safe from artillery fire.

**Dead Zones**:

Very close to gun positions, artillery could not fire (minimum range). But at Verdun, artillery was positioned far enough back that this was not a significant factor.

**Time of Flight**:

The time between firing and impact varied by range and shell:

- **75mm at 5 km range**: ~10-15 seconds flight time
- **155mm at 10 km range**: ~20-30 seconds flight time
- **305mm at 12 km range**: ~30-45 seconds flight time
- **420mm at 9 km range**: ~40-60 seconds flight time

**Implication for Soldiers**: Heavier shells had longer flight times, giving soldiers more warning to take cover—if they heard the shell coming and recognized it correctly.

### Barrage Patterns

**Trommelfeuer (Drumfire - German)**:
- Continuous saturation fire across a defined area
- Multiple batteries firing in sequence to maintain unbroken barrage
- Duration: Hours to days
- Purpose: Destroy defenses, break morale, suppress defenders

**Sperrfeuer (Barrage Fire - German)**:
- Linear barrage to isolate a sector
- Prevents reinforcements or retreat
- Purpose: Trap defenders in a zone

**Barrage Roulant (Rolling Barrage - French)**:
- Timed artillery fire advancing ahead of infantry assault
- Shells land 50-100 meters ahead of attacking troops
- Lifts forward in coordinated increments (e.g., advance 100 meters every 3 minutes)
- Purpose: Suppress defenders while infantry advances under protective curtain

Understanding artillery range and flight time helps players:

- Recognize shells by sound and estimate impact time

# SECTION III — LIFE AT VERDUN

This section connects the physical realities of Verdun to daily survival conditions. Each subsection explains a historical reality and shows how it translates into game mechanics. The purpose is to demonstrate that *Only The Dead* does not create arbitrary difficulty—it simulates documented conditions.

## CHAPTER 1: FOOD AND RATION SCARCITY → HUNGER SYSTEM

### The Official French Ration (1916)

French regulations specified a daily ration per soldier:

- Bread: 700g (or 400g hardtack if bread unavailable)
- Fresh or canned meat: 300g
- Dried vegetables: 100g (peas, beans, lentils)
- Wine: 250ml
- Coffee substitute: 24g (chicory or barley)
- Sugar: 32g
- Salt: 20g
- Tobacco: 50g per week

**Total caloric value**: 3,200-3,500 calories per day (on paper)

### The Reality at Verdun

**Good days (20% of time)**: Full ration arrived, relatively fresh. Morale boost.

**Average days (50% of time)**: 70-80% of ration arrived. Bread 2-3 days old, hard and moldy. Meat questionable (bulging cans, rancid smell). Wine arrived (critical for morale). Vegetables and extras missing.

**Bad days (25% of time)**: 40-60% of ration due to supply convoy shelling. Bread 4-5 days old (inedible parts cut away). Meat spoiled. No wine (morale disaster).

**Disaster days (5% of time)**: No ration delivery for 24-48 hours during major offensives. Men ate emergency reserves or scavenged from dead.

### Why Rations Failed to Arrive

- **Artillery fire**: Supply convoys hit while moving to front
- **Mud**: Trucks and carts bogged down
- **Breakdown**: Vehicles disabled
- **Priority shifts**: Ammunition took precedence over food during crises

### Caloric Deficit

**Frontline soldiers needed**: 4,000-4,500 calories/day
- Hard labor (digging, carrying, fighting)
- Cold weather (body burns more maintaining warmth)
- Stress (increases metabolic rate)

**What they received**:
- Good days: 3,000-3,200 calories (deficit)
- Average days: 2,400-2,800 calories (significant deficit)
- Bad days: 1,500-2,000 calories (severe deficit)
- Disaster days: 500-1,000 calories (starvation)

**Consequence**: Soldiers lost 5-10 kg during a 4-7 day front-line rotation. Constant hunger. Weakness. Slower healing. Increased disease susceptibility.

### Food Quality Issues

**Bread** (*Pain de Munition*):
- Arrived 2-5 days old
- Hard as a brick (couldn't bite into it)
- Moldy (green/white mold; men cut away worst parts)
- Weevils and maggots (picked out or ignored)
- Had to soak in coffee or soup to eat

**Hardtack** (*Biscuit de Guerre*):
- Rock-hard flour biscuits
- Lasted indefinitely but tasteless
- Required 15-20 minutes soaking to become edible
- Often dated from 1914-1915 (years old)

**Canned Meat** (*Singe* / "Monkey Meat"):
- Bulging cans = gas buildup from bacteria (botulism risk)
- Official rule: discard bulging cans
- Reality: ate them anyway (hunger > safety)
- Result: stomach cramps, diarrhea, sometimes death
- Rancid fat scraped off, meat beneath consumed

**Wine**:
- More important than extra food for morale
- Cheap, acidic, weak wine (*pinard*)
- Dulled fear, cold, pain
- Social bonding ritual
- When wine didn't arrive: near-mutiny

### How This Connects to the Game

**Hunger Meter (0-100)**:
- Depletes 3-8 points per hour depending on activity
- Below 40: Stamina penalties, slower movement
- Below 20: Severe stamina reduction, aim shake, vision blur
- 0: Collapse risk, death in 7 days

**Food Restoration Values**:
- Fresh bread (700g): +40 hunger
- Moldy bread (edible parts): +25 hunger
- Hardtack (soaked): +20 hunger
- Canned meat (good quality): +35 hunger
- Canned meat (questionable): +25 hunger + 20% sickness chance
- Wine (250ml): +5 hunger, +15 morale

**Ration Delivery System**:
- Random delivery success (influenced by artillery intensity, mission phase)
- Quality varies (fresh → stale → questionable → spoiled)
- Player must decide: eat spoiled food (risk sickness) or stay hungry?

**Cooking Mechanics**:
- Gathering firewood
- Starting fire (risk: smoke attracts artillery)
- Heating food improves quality and safety
- Communal cooking = morale boost for squad

### Historical Testimony

> "I dream of food. Not the food we eat here—the bread like rock, the meat that reeks. I dream of my mother's cooking. Fresh bread. Butter. Real coffee. I wake up and cry." — Maurice Genevoix, *Ceux de 14*

> "Hunger is constant. You're never full. Even on good days, you're still hungry. You think about food all the time. It's worse than the fear." — Anonymous soldier, Verdun 1916

## CHAPTER 2: WATER SCARCITY → HYDRATION SYSTEM

### The Critical Resource

Water was more critical than food. A soldier could survive a week without food. Without water, death came in 1-3 days.

**Official allowance**: 1 liter per man per day (drinking water) + additional for cooking (communal)

### Water Delivery Challenges

**Transportation**:
- Delivered in barrels or metal cans from rear areas
- Carried forward by supply details (*corvée*)
- Weight: 1 liter = 1 kg (heavy)
- Often spilled during transport
- Contaminated by shell fragments, dirt, rats

**Under Fire**:
- Water carriers exposed during movement
- Barrels hit by shrapnel (water lost)
- Men killed while carrying water

### Water Quality

**Clean water (rare)**: From springs or wells in rear areas, properly transported.

**Questionable water (common)**: From shell craters, contaminated streams.
- Tasted of mud, chemicals, decomposition
- Caused dysentery (diarrhea, dehydration, death)
- Boiled when possible (didn't eliminate all contamination)

**Desperate measures**:
- Drank from puddles
- Licked moisture from dugout walls
- Rainwater collection (if containers available)
- Some drank from water-filled shell craters (high contamination, often fatal)

### The Fort Vaux Water Crisis (June 1916)

During the 7-day siege, the garrison had approximately **2 liters of water per man TOTAL**—not per day, but for the entire week.

Men:
- Licked condensation from walls
- Drank their own urine
- Died of thirst faster than from wounds
- Became delirious from dehydration

When Major Raynal surrendered on June 7, German officers immediately provided water. This gesture was considered more humane than continued combat.

### Dehydration Effects

**Mild (Thirst 70-40)**:
- Dry mouth, thirst
- Reduced stamina regeneration
- Slight performance degradation

**Moderate (Thirst 39-20)**:
- Severe thirst, headache
- Reduced stamina maximum
- Vision blur
- Movement speed reduced
- Confusion begins

**Severe (Thirst 19-1)**:
- Delirium, hallucinations
- Extreme weakness
- Cannot perform complex tasks
- Collapse risk

**Critical (Thirst 0)**:
- Unconsciousness
- Death in 24-48 hours

### How This Connects to the Game

**Thirst Meter (0-100)**:
- Depletes 5-12 points per hour (faster in summer, during exertion)
- **Most critical meter** (faster death than hunger)
- Below 40: Stamina regen penalty, light aim shake
- Below 20: Severe stamina penalty, vision blur, headache visual effects
- 0: Collapse, death in 24 hours

**Water Sources**:
- Clean water (canteen refill): +50 thirst, safe
- Rainwater: +20 thirst, mostly safe
- Crater water: +15 thirst, 40% disease chance, 20% vomiting chance
- Desperate measures (licking walls): +5 thirst, narrative option

**Decision Points**:
- Drink contaminated water now (survive short-term, risk disease) or stay thirsty (die soon)?
- Share water with wounded comrade or keep for self?
- Risk exposure to refill canteen or ration remaining supply?

### Historical Testimony

> "I can live without food for a day. I cannot live without water. At Verdun, water is gold." — Soldier testimony

> "We drank from the crater. We knew it would make us sick. But thirst is worse than sickness." — Diary entry, April 1916

## CHAPTER 3: TRENCH FOOT, FROSTBITE, ROT → HEALTH DEGRADATION

### Trench Foot: The Epidemic

**Cause**: Prolonged exposure (12+ hours) to cold, wet conditions. Feet in mud and water constantly.

**Progression**:

**Stage 1 (12-24 hours)**:
- Feet numb, pale, cold
- Tingling sensation
- Reversible if dried and warmed

**Stage 2 (24-72 hours)**:
- Feet swollen, painful
- Skin white/gray, wrinkled
- Blisters forming
- Requires medical care

**Stage 3 (72+ hours)**:
- Tissue death (necrosis)
- Blackened toes/feet
- Gangrene (infection)
- Treatment: Amputation

**Statistics**: Thousands of cases at Verdun. 20-30% resulted in amputation. 5% died from infection.

### Prevention (Difficult at Verdun)

Official orders:
- Change socks daily (impossible—no dry socks)
- Dry feet when possible (everything was wet)
- Rub feet with whale oil or grease (limited supply)
- Rotate off front line before onset (noria helped but insufficient)

Reality: Prevention was mostly impossible.

### Frostbite (Winter 1916)

**Cause**: Prolonged exposure to freezing temperatures without adequate warmth.

**Effects**:
- Numb extremities (fingers, toes, nose, ears)
- Tissue damage
- Permanent loss of function
- Amputation if severe

**Prevention**: Warm clothing (often inadequate), movement (generates heat), shelter (dugouts slightly warmer).

### Hygiene Degradation

**The Mud**:
- Uniforms caked in mud (added 3-5 kg weight)
- Boots rotted from constant moisture
- Skin infections from constant dampness

**Lice** (Universal):
- All soldiers had lice (officers to privates)
- Hundreds to thousands per person
- Lived in clothing seams, body hair
- Caused constant itching, sleep deprivation
- Carried trench fever (bacterial infection, high fever, debilitating)

**Rats**:
- Millions of rats at Verdun
- Fed on corpses and ration scraps
- Rats as large as cats
- Bit sleeping men
- Carried diseases (leptospirosis, plague)

### Disease

**Dysentery**: Diarrhea from contaminated water/food. Dehydration. Weakness. Death if untreated.

**Trench Fever**: Lice-borne bacterial infection. High fever, severe leg pain, rash. Incapacitated for weeks.

**Respiratory Infections**: Pneumonia, bronchitis from cold, damp, crowded dugouts.

### How This Connects to the Game

**Hygiene Meter (0-100)**:
- Depletes 2 points/hour baseline (faster in combat, summer)
- Below 40: Disease risk, morale penalty, lice infestation
- Below 20: High disease risk (dysentery, trench fever), severe morale penalty
- 0: Illness guaranteed

**Trench Foot System**:
- Time in water/mud tracked
- Warning progression: feet numb (stage 1) → painful (stage 2) → critical (stage 3)
- Stage 3 = medical evacuation required or permanent movement speed reduction
- Prevention: dry feet when possible, change position, rest

**Disease Effects**:
- Dysentery: -60% stamina, +200% thirst depletion, duration 5 days
- Trench Fever: High fever hallucinations, -50% all stats, duration 7 days
- Lice: Constant minor morale penalty, itching (camera shake), disease vector

**Hygiene Actions**:
- Wash face/hands: +5 hygiene
- Full wash (if water available): +15 hygiene
- Change clothing (rare): +20 hygiene
- De-lousing (manual): +10 hygiene, social activity with squad

### Historical Testimony

> "The lice are my constant companions. I've named some of them. At night, when I can't sleep, I hunt them by candlelight. It's something to do." — Louis Barthas, 1916

> "My feet haven't been dry in three days. I can't feel my toes anymore. I'm scared they'll turn black." — Letter home, March 1916

## CHAPTER 4: GAS EXPOSURE → MASK AND BREATHING DISCIPLINE

### Gas Weapons at Verdun

**Types Used**:

**Chlorine** (early war, less common by 1916):
- Yellow-green cloud
- Odor: bleach
- Effect: Lung irritant, burning eyes, suffocation
- Treatment: Move to fresh air, rest

**Phosgene** (Green Cross shells, primary at Verdun):
- White mist
- Odor: musty hay
- Effect: Damages lung alveoli, pulmonary edema (fluid in lungs)
- Latency: 24-48 hours (victim feels fine initially, then drowns internally)
- Lethality: Heavy exposure = 80-90% fatal
- Treatment: None effective in 1916

**Diphosgene** (concentrated phosgene):
- Similar to phosgene but more concentrated
- Used in June 1916 attacks

**Arsenic Irritant** (Blue Cross shells):
- Induces vomiting, forces mask removal
- Combined with lethal gas (Blue Cross first, then Green Cross)
- Tactic: Make victims remove masks, then kill with poison gas

### Gas Shell Recognition

**Sound**:
- Similar flight sound to HE shells (whistle or rumble)
- Impact: Distinctive soft "pop" or "plop" (NOT loud explosion)
- Shell designed to break open and release gas, not explode
- After impact: Hissing sound (gas escaping)

**Visual**:
- Cloud formation (color depends on type)
- Gas heavier than air (sinks into trenches, fills craters and dugouts)

### Gas Mask Discipline

**Immediate Action**:
1. **Shout warning**: "*Gaz! Gaz!*" (alert others)
2. **Don mask**: 10-30 seconds to put on mask (practice made fast)
3. **Stay low**: Gas sinks (crouch or lie down)
4. **Breathe slowly**: Through mask filter (panic = hyperventilation = mask failure)
5. **Wait**: Gas dissipates in 10-30 minutes depending on wind

**Mask Types**:
- M2 mask (early 1916): Cloth mask soaked in chemicals, limited protection
- ARS mask (improved, mid-1916): Rubber face piece, canister filter, better protection

**Survival**:
- With mask (donned in time): Low lethality
- Without mask or donned too late: High lethality (death in 2-10 minutes)
- Mask failure (damage, improper seal): Usually fatal

### The Horror of Gas Wounds

Phosgene caused pulmonary edema: lungs filled with fluid, victims drowned in their own body fluids. Symptoms:
- Coughing, choking
- Frothy pink sputum
- Asphyxiation
- Death over hours to days

No effective treatment existed. Morphine eased suffering.

### How This Connects to the Game

**Gas Detection**:
- Auditory cue: Soft "pop" impact sound (distinct from HE shells)
- Visual cue: Cloud formation, color
- NPC shouts: "*Gaz! Gaz!*"
- Time to react: 5-15 seconds

**Gas Mask System**:
- Mask equip time: 10 seconds (button prompt)
- If masked in time: Survive (vision restricted through mask)
- If too late: Rapid health loss, death in 1-2 minutes
- Mask damage: Can be damaged in combat, must repair or find replacement

**Breathing Discipline**:
- Stamina drains faster while masked (restricted breathing)
- Cannot sprint while masked
- Must remove mask to eat/drink (risk if gas still present)
- Duration: Must wait for gas to dissipate (10-30 minutes)

**Gas Wounds** (if exposed without mask):
- Immediate: Coughing, vision blur, health drain
- Latent (phosgene): Feel fine for hours, then sudden health collapse (requires medical evacuation)
- Treatment: None available at front; morphine only (palliative)

### Historical Testimony

> "The gas came like a wall. We shouted '*Gaz!*' and grabbed our masks. I got mine on. My comrade didn't. He clawed at his throat. There was nothing I could do." — Testimony, June 1916

> "Phosgene is the worst. You breathe it and think you're fine. Then, a day later, you drown. And there's no stopping it." — Medical officer report

## CHAPTER 5: SLEEP DEPRIVATION → STAMINA AND TREMOR EFFECTS

### Sleep Reality at Verdun

**Official need**: 8 hours sleep per night

**Actual sleep at front**:
- Front line: 2-3 hours/night (broken into fragments)
- Support line: 4-6 hours/night
- Rest area: 8-10 hours/night (first time in weeks)

### Why Sleep Was Impossible

**Noise**:
- Artillery rumble (constant, 24/7)
- Close explosions (jarring wake-ups)
- Machine guns (distant staccato)
- Voices, screaming, orders

**Discomfort**:
- Cold, wet ground
- Hard surfaces (no mattresses)
- Cramped dugouts (sit hunched or lie down)
- Mud (everything soaked)

**Pests**:
- Rats running over sleeping men
- Lice itching constantly

**Fear**:
- Anticipating bombardment, attack, death
- Nightmares (shell shock symptoms)

**Duty**:
- Sentry rotations (2-hour shifts; sleep interrupted)
- Stand-to (dawn and dusk alerts; everyone awake)
- Working parties (night labor; no sleep)

### Sleep Deprivation Effects

**Physical**:
- Exhaustion
- Slower reactions
- Weakened immune system (more disease)
- Tremors (hands shake)

**Cognitive**:
- Impaired judgment (dangerous decisions)
- Micro-sleep (falling asleep standing up or during tasks)
- Hallucinations (seeing/hearing things that aren't there)
- Confusion, disorientation

**Psychological**:
- Irritability
- Depression
- Anxiety
- Increased shell shock susceptibility

### Cumulative Debt

After 7 days of inadequate sleep, soldiers approached collapse. The noria rotation system attempted to prevent this by cycling divisions off the front every 7-14 days, but damage accumulated over months.

### How This Connects to the Game

**Alertness Meter (0-100)**:
- Depletes 4 points/hour while awake (faster at night, on sentry duty)
- Restored by sleep (25 points/hour slept, but sleep is interrupted)
- Below 40: Slower reactions, reduced aim speed
- Below 20: Vision blur, micro-sleep risk (screen fades briefly)
- 0: Fall asleep immediately (dangerous—wake only if shelled or attacked)

**Sleep Mechanics**:
- Location affects sleep quality:
  - Open trench: 30% restoration (interrupted by noise, exposure)
  - Shallow dugout: 50% restoration
  - Deep dugout: 70% restoration
  - Rest area: 100% restoration
- Random interruptions: artillery, rats, duty call, nightmares
- Cannot control when sleep ends (realistic unpredictability)

**Sleep Deprivation Consequences**:
- Cumulative debt: After 7 days below 40 alertness average = collapse risk
- Hallucinations (low alertness): Brief visual/auditory distortions
- Tremors: Hands shake (aim wobble increases)

### Historical Testimony

> "I'm so tired I could sleep standing up. And sometimes I do. You learn to sleep with your eyes open, leaning against the trench wall. You never sleep deeply. You're always half-awake, waiting for the shell that kills you." — Maurice Genevoix

> "Sleep is a luxury. At Verdun, you forget what it feels like to wake up rested." — Veteran testimony

## CHAPTER 6: PSYCHOLOGICAL STRAIN → PANIC AND TUNNEL VISION

### The Mental Reality

Verdun was not just physical survival—it was psychological endurance against constant, random death.

### Sources of Psychological Strain

**Randomness of Death**:
- Artillery killed randomly
- No way to predict where shells landed
- No way to fight back against artillery
- Survival was luck, not skill

**Helplessness**:
- Could not control when bombardment started or stopped
- Could not see the enemy (guns kilometers away)
- Could not escape (trapped in sector until rotation)

**Constant Exposure**:
- 303 days of combat
- Some soldiers at Verdun for months
- No reprieve, only brief rotations to rear

**Witnessing Death**:
- Comrades killed instantly (obliterated by shells)
- Wounded dying slowly
- Corpses everywhere (unburied, decomposing)
- Rats eating corpses

**Sensory Overload**:
- Constant noise (never silent)
- Smell of death, gas, decay
- Visual horror (dismembered bodies, destroyed landscape)

### Shell Shock (*Obusite*, French Medical Term)

**Symptoms**:
- Tremors (uncontrollable shaking)
- Mutism (cannot speak)
- Paralysis (psychological, not physical injury)
- Deafness (psychological or eardrums damaged)
- Nightmares, flashbacks
- Dissociation (stare into space, unresponsive)
- Panic attacks

**Causes**:
- Prolonged bombardment exposure
- Near-misses (concussion without visible wounds)
- Witnessing comrades killed
- Cumulative stress (no single event, just too much over time)

**Treatment (1916)**:
- Primitive: Rest in hospital, sometimes returned to front (not recovered)
- Stigma: Initially called "coward's disease"
- Recognition: Gradually accepted as medical condition (mid-war)

**Prevalence**: Thousands of cases at Verdun. Many undiagnosed or untreated.

### Coping Mechanisms

**Fatalism**:
- "If the shell has my name, I'll die. If not, I won't. No use worrying."
- Surrender to randomness (psychological relief)

**Superstition**:
- Lucky charms (religious medals, talismans)
- Rituals (prayers, specific routines)

**Humor**:
- Dark jokes about death
- Laughter as release

**Routine**:
- Focus on duties (maintenance, cooking, writing letters)
- Keep mind occupied
- Sense of control

**Camaraderie**:
- Talk with comrades (shared experience, mutual support)
- Physical presence (not alone)

**Dissociation**:
- Mental shutdown (stop feeling fear)
- Automatic actions (like a robot)
- Emotional numbness (protective mechanism)

### Progression Over Time

**Week 1**: Terror (every shell = panic)
**Week 2**: Adaptation begins (recognize patterns, sounds)
**Weeks 3-4**: Numbness (dissociation, fatalism)
**After 1 month**: Breakdown (shell shock) OR complete adaptation (veteran)

### How This Connects to the Game

**Morale Meter (0-100)**:
- Depletes 0.5 points/hour baseline
- Events affect morale:
  - Friendly death witnessed: -15
  - Artillery near-miss: -8
  - Corpse encountered: -3
  - Letter from home: +20
  - Hot meal: +8
  - Rotation to rest: +25
- Below 40: Increased shell shock risk, aim shake
- Below 20: Panic risk, severe combat effectiveness penalty
- 0: Breakdown (temporary loss of player control, must recover)

**Shell Shock System**:
- Trigger: Morale < 30 AND traumatic event (close shell hit, witnessing death)
- Symptoms:
  - Tremors (severe aim shake)
  - Vision tunneling
  - Audio muffling
  - Slower reactions
  - Temporary inability to perform complex actions
- Recovery: Rest, time away from front, medical care

**Panic Effects**:
- High morale: Calm under fire, steady aim
- Low morale: Panic during bombardment (loss of control briefly), shaky hands, tunnel vision

**Coping Interactions**:
- Write letter home (morale +10, activity takes time)
- Talk with comrades (morale +5, bonding)
- Dark humor dialogue options (morale +5, player choice)

### Historical Testimony

> "Every shell, you think: Is this mine? If it lands on you, you never know. One second you exist. The next, you don't." — Veteran testimony

> "I stopped feeling fear after a month. I stopped feeling anything. I was a ghost walking through Hell." — Shell shock survivor

> "The sound never stops. Even now, years later, I hear it. The rumble. The whistles. I'll hear it until I die." — Post-war veteran interview

# SECTION IV — BATTLE TIMELINE (CONDENSED, FACTUAL)

This section provides a chronological overview of the Battle of Verdun's key phases. Each entry is factual, verified from historical records, and condensed for clarity.

---

## PHASE I: THE STORM BREAKS (February 21 - March 6, 1916)

### February 21, 1916 — Opening Bombardment

**07:15**: German artillery begins. 1,220 guns fire approximately 1,000,000 shells over 9 hours along a 20km front.

**16:00**: Infantry assault begins (delayed by snow). German forces advance with flamethrowers.

**Target**: Bois des Caures, defended by Colonel Émile Driant's chasseurs.

### February 22, 1916 — Driant's Death

Colonel Driant killed during German assault. Bois des Caures falls. French 51st Division suffers 60% casualties.

### February 25, 1916 — Fall of Fort Douaumont

**16:00**: Fort Douaumont captured by small German detachment. The fort was garrisoned by only 56 territorial artillerists—no combat troops. French national shock. German propaganda triumph.

### February 26, 1916 — Pétain Arrives

General Philippe Pétain appointed commander, Second Army at Verdun. Immediate actions:
- Reorganize artillery (centralized command)
- Establish noria (division rotation system)
- Restore supply line (La Voie Sacrée—The Sacred Way)
- Order: "*Ils ne passeront pas*" (They shall not pass)

### March 6, 1916 — German Pause

Crown Prince Wilhelm orders operational pause to consolidate gains. German advance: ~10km depth, 25km² area captured. French line stabilized under Pétain.

---

## PHASE II: LEFT BANK OFFENSIVE (March 6 - April 9, 1916)

### March 6, 1916 — Battle for Mort-Homme Begins

German offensive expands to left (west) bank of Meuse. Target: Hill 295 ("Le Mort-Homme" / Dead Man's Hill) to eliminate French artillery observation.

### March-April 1916 — The Meat Grinder

Continuous rain turns battlefield to liquid mud. Trenches collapse. Wounded drown in shell craters. Casualty rate: ~25,000 per week (both sides combined). No significant territorial gains—pure attrition.

---

## PHASE III: THE MAY INFERNO (April 9 - June 7, 1916)

### May 3, 1916 — Hill 304 Falls

After 7 weeks of fighting, Germans capture Hill 304. Cost: ~20,000 German casualties, ~30,000 French.

### May 20, 1916 — Germans Capture Mort-Homme Peak

French retain southern slopes. Tactical stalemate but psychological blow.

### June 1-7, 1916 — Siege of Fort Vaux

**June 1**: German bombardment begins (500 guns, 8,000 shells/day for 5 days).

**June 2**: Infantry assault. German flamethrowers in tunnels. Hand-to-hand combat in darkness.

**June 3-6**: Water cistern destroyed. Garrison of ~600 men under Major Raynal has ~2 liters of water per man TOTAL for 7 days. Men lick walls, drink urine, die of thirst.

**June 4**: Carrier pigeon "Vaillant" delivers final message (bird wounded but reaches French lines).

**June 7**: Raynal surrenders. German officers provide water immediately and grant honors for defense.

---

## PHASE IV: THE HIGH WATER MARK (June 8 - June 23, 1916)

### June 21-23, 1916 — Farthest German Advance

**June 23**: German forces reach Fleury-devant-Douaumont, **4km from Verdun city**. French line near collapse.

Crown Prince requests final assault for June 24. Chief of Staff Falkenhayn refuses—troops exhausted.

**July 1**: Battle of the Somme begins (British-French offensive). German reserves diverted. Verdun offensive halted.

---

## PHASE V: THE GREAT ATTRITION (June 23 - October 24, 1916)

### July-August 1916 — Stalemate

Summer heat. Decomposing bodies. Flies, disease, dysentery epidemic. Artillery duels continue daily.

**Notable**: Thiaumont Ouvrage changes hands **16 times** (June-August). Fleury village reduced to dust, changes hands repeatedly.

### September-October 1916 — French Prepare

General Nivelle plans counteroffensive. French artillery reorganized and reinforced. German strength declining (Somme casualties mount).

---

## PHASE VI: THE FRENCH RECLAMATION (October 24 - December 18, 1916)

### October 24, 1916 — Counteroffensive Begins

**Commander**: General Charles Mangin (under Nivelle).

**Preparation**: 740 French guns, 4-day bombardment, creeping barrage tactics, colonial assault troops (Moroccan Division).

**11:40**: Infantry assault. Rapid advance through devastated terrain.

**By evening**: **Fort Douaumont liberated**. 6,000 German prisoners.

### November 2, 1916 — Fort Vaux Liberated

Germans evacuate Fort Vaux during night (Nov 1-2). Position untenable, supply lines cut. French reoccupy without resistance.

### December 15, 1916 — Final Offensive

French push Germans back to approximately February 21 starting lines.

### December 18, 1916 — Battle Ends

**Result**: French strategic and symbolic victory. Douaumont and Vaux recaptured. Verdun city held. German offensive failed.

**Cost**: 303 days, ~714,000 casualties, front line restored to original positions.

# SECTION VI — GAME MECHANICS EXPLAINED THROUGH REAL CONTEXT

This section explains why *Only The Dead* gameplay systems work the way they do, grounded in historical reality.

---

## 1. STAMINA AND EXHAUSTION → TRENCH LABOR + SLEEP LOSS

**Historical Reality**:

Soldiers at Verdun performed exhausting labor daily:
- Digging trenches and dugouts (6-8 hours shifts)
- Carrying supplies (25-40kg loads through mud)
- Repairing damaged positions (constant)
- Sentry duty (2-hour rotations, no rest)
- Combat operations (when attacked)

Sleep averaged 2-3 hours per night at front line. Chronic sleep deprivation accumulated over 7-14 day rotations.

**Caloric Deficit**: Soldiers needed 4,000-4,500 cal/day but received 2,400-3,200 cal/day average.

**Game Implementation**:

**Stamina Meter**:
- Depletes during all physical actions (movement, climbing, carrying, combat)
- Regenerates slowly (realistic recovery time)
- Affected by hunger, thirst, sleep (cascading penalties)

**Why It Matters**:
- Cannot sprint indefinitely (realistic exhaustion)
- Must manage energy (choose when to move quickly vs conserve)
- Carrying wounded drains stamina rapidly (authentic difficulty)

**Historical Accuracy**: Veterans reported constant exhaustion. Simple tasks became monumental efforts. This is simulated.

---

## 2. AIM SWAY → STARVATION, FATIGUE, COLD TREMOR

**Historical Reality**:

Factors causing hand tremors and aim instability:
- **Starvation**: Muscle weakness from caloric deficit
- **Fatigue**: Sleep deprivation caused tremors
- **Cold**: Hypothermia caused uncontrollable shaking
- **Shell Shock**: Psychological trauma caused physical tremors
- **Fear**: Adrenaline response caused shaky hands

**Game Implementation**:

**Aim Sway Increases When**:
- Hunger < 40: Light shake (muscle weakness)
- Stamina < 30: Moderate shake (exhaustion)
- Warmth < 20: Severe shake (cold tremor)
- Morale < 30: Severe shake (fear, shell shock)
- Alertness < 20: Moderate shake (fatigue)

**Why It Matters**:
- Shooting accurately requires good physical/mental state
- Managing survival meters directly impacts combat effectiveness
- Reflects reality that combat capability depended on basic needs being met

**Historical Testimony**: "My hands shook so badly I could barely hold my rifle. Cold, hunger, fear—all of it. I couldn't hit anything beyond 50 meters." — Soldier account

---

## 3. SHELL SHOCK BLUR → CONCUSSION AND PRESSURE WAVE EFFECTS

**Historical Reality**:

Artillery concussion caused:
- **Blast Wave Trauma**: Pressure waves ruptured organs, caused internal bleeding
- **Concussion**: Unconsciousness, confusion, disorientation
- **Tinnitus**: Ringing ears (permanent damage)
- **Vision Disturbance**: Blurred vision, tunnel vision, temporary blindness
- **Shell Shock**: Psychological trauma manifesting as physical symptoms

Even near-misses (shells landing 10-20 meters away) caused concussion effects.

**Game Implementation**:

**After Artillery Near-Miss**:
- Vision blur (simulates concussion)
- Audio muffling + tinnitus (high-pitched ringing)
- Movement impaired briefly (disorientation)
- Cannot aim accurately (shaking, confusion)
- Duration: 30-60 seconds (recovers gradually)

**After Severe Concussion**:
- Blackout (unconsciousness)
- Wake up disoriented
- Extended impairment (minutes)

**Why It Matters**:
- Artillery is dangerous even without direct hit
- Simulates helplessness soldiers felt
- Creates authentic fear of bombardment

---

## 4. WOUND PERSISTENCE → WWI FIELD MEDICINE LIMITATIONS

**Historical Reality**:

WWI field medicine had severe limitations:
- **No Antibiotics**: Infections were common and often fatal (penicillin not available until 1928)
- **Limited Surgery**: Amputations common, complex surgery often impossible
- **Slow Evacuation**: Hours to reach field hospital (infection set in)
- **Pain Management**: Morphine available but limited
- **Wounds Didn't Heal Quickly**: Shrapnel wounds took weeks to heal if they didn't become infected

**Game Implementation**:

**Wounds Persist**:
- Light wounds (shrapnel): Bleeding, pain, reduced movement until bandaged
- Moderate wounds: Require medical attention (aid station)
- Severe wounds: Require evacuation (mission ends or character dies)
- Infection risk if wounds not treated properly

**No Instant Healing**:
- Bandages stop bleeding but don't restore health
- Must rest/eat/seek medical care to recover
- Some wounds cause permanent penalties until mission end

**Why It Matters**:
- Creates tension (wounded = vulnerable)
- Encourages avoidance of combat (getting shot is serious)
- Reflects reality that survival depended on avoiding injury

---

## 5. ARTILLERY DENSITY → REAL SHELL-PER-SECOND BOMBARDMENT DATA

**Historical Reality**:

Artillery density at Verdun:
- **Opening bombardment (Feb 21)**: 1,000,000 shells in 9 hours = ~30 shells per second across 20km front
- **Average day**: 130,000 shells = ~1.5 shells per second (all day)
- **Peak bombardments**: 200,000+ shells/day = ~2-3 shells per second

Soldiers experienced constant, random explosions. No pattern. No safety.

**Game Implementation**:

**Artillery Frequency**:
- **Harassment fire** (background): 5-10 shells/hour randomly placed
- **Scheduled bombardment**: 30-60 shells/minute in player's sector
- **Major offensive bombardment**: 120+ shells/minute (survival requires deep dugout)

**Why It Matters**:
- Simulates constant danger
- No "safe" moment (random death possible anytime)
- Creates psychological tension matching historical accounts

---

## 6. INDIRECT FIRE LETHALITY → EXPOSURE OVER MARKSMANSHIP

**Historical Reality**:

Cause of death at Verdun:
- **Artillery**: 70-75% of casualties
- **Small arms**: 15-20% of casualties
- **Gas**: 3-5%
- **Other**: 5-10%

**Why Artillery Dominated**:
- Soldiers spent 90% of time in trenches (cover from rifles)
- Machine guns prevented open movement
- Artillery reached men in trenches
- Death was random (exposure, not skill-based)

**Game Implementation**:

**Combat is Defensive**:
- Player rarely engages in sustained firefights
- Most deaths from artillery (authentic)
- Rifle combat only when forced (enemy assault, patrol encounter)
- Survival = avoid exposure, not win gunfights

**Why It Matters**:
- Reflects historical reality (WWI was not about marksmanship)
- Creates different gameplay feel (survival horror, not shooter)
- Honors truth of experience (soldiers felt helpless against artillery)

# SECTION VII — SURVIVAL & FIELD CRAFT (REAL TECHNIQUES)

This section teaches practical survival techniques soldiers used at Verdun.

---

## 1. MOVEMENT UNDER FIRE

**When to Move**:
- Between bombardments (lulls)
- During night (reduced visibility)
- During fog/rain (visual cover)
- When absolutely necessary (risk vs reward)

**When to Stay Still**:
- During active bombardment (movement attracts attention)
- When shells incoming (find nearest cover, don't run)
- When enemy has visual contact (movement = target)

**How to Move**:
- **Crouch**: Lower profile, harder to see
- **Sprint only when necessary**: Exhausting, attracts attention
- **Use terrain**: Craters, trenches, ruins for cover
- **Move in short bursts**: Run-crouch-observe-run
- **Never cross open ground in daylight**: Suicidal

---

## 2. COVER VS CONCEALMENT UNDER ARTILLERY

**Cover** (Physical Protection):
- **Deep dugout**: Best (survives heavy shells)
- **Shallow dugout**: Good (survives light/medium shells)
- **Trench**: Moderate (protection from shrapnel, not HE)
- **Shell crater**: Temporary (can be hit again)
- **Open ground**: None (death if close hit)

**Concealment** (Visual Hiding):
- **Smoke**: Hides from observers but doesn't stop shells
- **Night**: Reduces visual targeting
- **Fog/Rain**: Natural concealment

**Key Understanding**: Concealment doesn't stop artillery. Only physical cover protects. Prioritize cover.

---

## 3. LISTENING FOR INCOMING VS OUTGOING SHELL SIGNATURES

**Outgoing** (Friendly Artillery):
- **Sound**: Boom behind you (gun firing)
- **Direction**: Sound travels away
- **Whistle**: Ascending pitch (shell leaving)
- **Response**: Ignore (not dangerous to you)

**Incoming** (Enemy Artillery):
- **Sound**: Whistle or rumble approaching (shell arriving)
- **Direction**: Sound getting louder (coming toward you)
- **Pitch**: Descending (shell descending)
- **Response**: Take cover immediately

**Shell Type Recognition** (by sound):
- **75mm**: High whistle, short duration (1-3 sec warning)
- **155mm**: Low rumble, medium duration (3-6 sec)
- **305mm**: Deep roar, long duration (10-15 sec)
- **Gas shell**: Soft "pop" on impact (distinctive, immediate gas alert)

**Practice**: Veterans could identify shell type and estimate landing point by sound alone. This skill saved lives.

---

## 4. GAS MASK DISCIPLINE

**Immediate Actions on Gas Alert**:
1. **Shout**: "*Gaz! Gaz!*" (warn others)
2. **Hold breath**: Don't inhale while donning mask
3. **Don mask**: 10-second drill (practiced constantly)
4. **Check seal**: Ensure tight fit (leaks = death)
5. **Breathe slowly**: Controlled breathing through filter

**Mask Maintenance**:
- Check filter regularly (replace if damaged)
- Keep mask accessible at all times (never pack it away)
- Practice donning (muscle memory in panic)

**Common Mistakes** (Fatal):
- **Panic**: Hyperventilating = faster gas intake
- **Removing mask too soon**: Gas takes 10-30 min to dissipate
- **Poor seal**: Facial hair, damaged rubber, improper fit

---

## 5. WHEN TO MOVE VS WHEN TO WAIT

**Move When**:
- Ordered to (assault, retreat, rotation)
- Position collapsing (trench destroyed, overrun)
- Medical emergency (evacuate wounded)
- Supply run (necessary risk)

**Wait When**:
- Under bombardment (movement = exposure)
- Enemy observing (visible = targeted)
- No clear objective (unnecessary risk)
- Night patrol not yet ready (patience)

**Judgment**: Soldiers learned to assess risk. Moving without cause = death. Waiting when action required = death. Experience taught the difference.

---

## 6. NIGHT MOVEMENT AND SOUND DISCIPLINE

**Why Night Movement**:
- Reduced visibility (enemy can't see you)
- Lower artillery risk (observers can't target precisely)
- Patrol activity (reconnaissance, wire repair, supply)

**Technique**:
- **Move slowly**: Feel ground before stepping (avoid noise)
- **No talking**: Hand signals only
- **No light**: Cigarettes, lamps forbidden (visible km away)
- **Listen constantly**: Hear enemy before seeing them
- **Stay low**: Silhouette visible against skyline

**Dangers**:
- **Barbed wire**: Nearly invisible at night (entanglement = exposure)
- **Shell craters**: Trip hazards, water-filled (drowning risk)
- **Friendly fire**: Own sentries might shoot unidentified movement
- **Flares**: Enemy illumination reveals position

**Sound Discipline**:
- No metal-on-metal (rifle clinks, equipment jingles)
- Wrap equipment (cloth around canteen, bayonet)
- Soft steps (avoid breaking sticks, splashing)
- Controlled breathing (heavy breathing audible in quiet)

---

## 7. THE IMPORTANCE OF STAYING WITH OTHERS

**Why Groups Survive**:
- **Mutual support**: Share labor, watch for danger
- **Medical aid**: Wounded comrades can help each other
- **Morale**: Less fear when not alone
- **Efficiency**: Divide tasks (one watches, one works)

**Why Individuals Die**:
- **No backup**: Wounded or trapped = death
- **Psychological collapse**: Isolation increases shell shock risk
- **Missed warnings**: No one to alert you to danger
- **Lost**: Disorientation in destroyed terrain

**Squad Cohesion**:
- Stay within visual/audio contact
- Account for everyone before moving
- Never leave wounded alone (unless ordered)
- Share resources (food, water, ammunition)

**Historical Reality**: Soldiers clung to their sections and squads. The bond was survival. Isolation was death.

# SECTION VIII — THE PLAYER'S EMOTIONAL ORIENTATION

This section prepares you for the intended experience of *Only The Dead*.

---

## 1. MOST DEATH IS INDIRECT AND UNSEEN

You will not see the enemy who kills you.

Artillery fires from kilometers away. The gunner does not see you. You do not see him. A shell lands. You die. Or you don't. It is random.

This is frustrating. It is supposed to be.

Verdun was frustrating. Soldiers could not fight back against artillery. They could only endure. The game reflects this reality.

**What This Means for You**:
- Death will feel unfair (it was unfair)
- You cannot "win" against artillery (accurate)
- Survival is sometimes luck (historically true)

**Why This Matters**: To honor the experience. Those men had no control. The game asks you to feel that helplessness, briefly, safely.

---

## 2. SURVIVAL IS SOMETIMES QUIET, SOMETIMES CHAOS

Some missions are quiet. You wait in a dugout during bombardment. You manage hunger and thirst. You talk with NPCs. Time passes slowly.

Other missions are chaos. Shells fall constantly. You run between craters. Wounded men scream. Everything is noise and terror.

Both are accurate.

**What This Means for You**:
- Not every moment is "exciting" (authentic pacing)
- Quiet moments build tension (waiting is its own horror)
- Chaos moments are overwhelming (sensory overload intentional)

**Why This Matters**: Verdun was hours of boredom punctuated by moments of terror. The game replicates this rhythm.

---

## 3. HEROIC ACTION IS NOT REWARDED; ENDURANCE IS

You will not save France. You will not turn the tide. You are one soldier among hundreds of thousands.

Your goal is simpler: Survive until relieved.

**What This Means for You**:
- No hero moments (realistic scope)
- Victory = staying alive (accurate definition)
- Endurance is the achievement (thematic truth)

**Why This Matters**: The game respects what those men actually did. They endured. That was heroic enough.

---

## 4. THE "FIGHT" IS PERSISTENCE, NOT VICTORY

You will not win battles. The battle is already written. History happened.

Your fight is against:
- Hunger
- Thirst
- Cold
- Fear
- Mud
- Artillery
- Time itself

**What This Means for You**:
- Manage survival meters (core gameplay)
- Make difficult choices (share food or keep it? Drink contaminated water or stay thirsty?)
- Accept losses (squad mates will die; you might too)

**Why This Matters**: The game asks: Can you persist? Can you endure what they endured, even briefly, even in simulation?

That is the question. The answer reveals something about the human capacity for endurance.

---

## FINAL WORDS

*Only The Dead* is not entertainment in the traditional sense. It is an experience. It is educational. It is a memorial.

714,000 men were casualties at Verdun. They cannot speak. This game speaks for them, through simulation, through fact, through respect.

When you play, remember: This happened. These conditions were real. These choices were real. These deaths were real.

You will emerge from the game. They did not.

That is the difference. Honor it.
