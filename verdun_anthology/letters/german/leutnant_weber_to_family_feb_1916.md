## Letter Content

**23. Februar 1916**
**Bei Douaumont**

Meine lieben Eltern und Geschwister,

I write this by candlelight in a dugout that reeks of cordite and unwashed men. We have been in the assault for two days, and already it feels like a lifetime.

The bombardment before the attack was beyond anything I have seen, even in the East. For nine hours, our artillery pounded the French positions. The earth itself seemed to scream. I thought surely nothing could survive such Hell, but when we went over the top on the 21st, the French were still there. Still shooting. Still fighting.

My company lost 40 men in the first day. Good men. Men I trained with, ate with, laughed with. Now they lie in the mud between the lines, and I must write letters to their families saying they died for the Fatherland. What comfort is that to a mother who has lost her son?

We captured the great fort - Douaumont - yesterday. A handful of our soldiers simply walked in and found it nearly empty. The French had abandoned their strongest position. We expected a fortress battle; we found a ghost. Now high command celebrates a great victory, but the men wonder: if they gave up Douaumont without a fight, what trap are they preparing?

I am so tired, Vater. Not just in body, but in soul. When I close my eyes I see the faces of the French soldiers we killed. They are young, like my men. They have mothers and sisters too. They do not want to be here any more than we do. Yet here we are, killing each other over these cursed hills.

The High Command promises this operation will end the war in the West. They say the French will bleed to death defending Verdun, and then we can turn all our strength against Russia. Perhaps they are right. But I look at my exhausted men, and I wonder who will bleed to death first.

Mutti, thank you for the package. The warm socks are a blessing. The chocolate I shared with my men - it reminded us that there is still sweetness in the world, even if we cannot taste it here.

To my sisters: Do not worry for me. I am as safe as any man can be in this place. Say a prayer for me, and for my men, and for our enemies too. We are all lost in the same nightmare.

The candle grows short. I must sleep while I can. Tomorrow we attack again.

Your son and brother,
Karl

P.S. - If this is my last letter, know that I died doing my duty, but wishing I was home.

## Historical Analysis

**Authentic Elements** (based on actual German letters):

## Comparison to French Perspective

| Aspect | French Perspective | German Perspective |
|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Defensive vs. Offensive** | Defending homeland | Attacking for strategic goals |
| **Morale** | "Ils ne passeront pas" defiance | Exhaustion + duty |
| **View of Enemy** | "Boche" but also human | "Franzosen" but also human |
| **Expectations** | Survival, hold position | Victory, end war quickly |
| **Reality** | Grinding defense, high cost | Grinding attack, high cost |

**Conclusion**: Both sides experienced similar horror, just from different tactical positions. Neither wanted to be there. Both bled for hills that meant nothing in the end.
