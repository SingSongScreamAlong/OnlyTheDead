# LETTER: Infirmière Marie Dubois to Sister
**From**: Marie Dubois, Infirmière volontaire (Volunteer Nurse), Ambulance 5/14
**To**: Mlle. Claire Dubois, Lyon
**Date**: April 18, 1916
**Location**: Bar-le-Duc Field Hospital
**Status**: Mailed, received May 1, 1916
**Censor Mark**: None (medical personnel correspondence less restricted)

---

My dearest Claire,

Forgive me for not writing sooner. I know you worry when weeks pass without word, but I simply have not had the strength to hold a pen. Now I have two hours—blessed, miraculous two hours—and I will use them to write to you, though I fear what I write will not bring you comfort.

I have been at Verdun for two months. When I volunteered in February, I thought I understood what I was offering to do. I had read about the wounded. I had imagined blood and pain and suffering. I thought myself prepared.

Claire, I was not prepared. No one could be.

---

The hospital is housed in what was once a school. There are beds for 200. We have 400 men. They lie in the corridors, in the classrooms, in the courtyard when the weather permits. They arrive in waves—trucks and ambulances disgorging their cargo of broken bodies. Sometimes we receive thirty in an hour. Sometimes a hundred.

I work eighteen hours a day. Sometimes twenty. There is always someone calling for water, for morphine, for their mother. There is always someone dying.

The wounds, Claire... I cannot describe them. I will try, because you asked me to tell you the truth, but I warn you: this is not the truth of newspapers. This is the truth of flesh.

**Shrapnel wounds**: Imagine a body that has been struck by dozens of metal fragments traveling at the speed of a bullet. The metal tears through skin, muscle, bone. It carries with it dirt and uniform scraps and bacteria. The wounds become infected almost immediately. We clean them, we cut away the dead tissue (they call it *débridement*—such a gentle word for such butchery), we irrigate with antiseptic solution. Many still die. The infection spreads faster than we can cut.

**Gas wounds**: These are the worst. Men arrive hours after exposure, sometimes a day. They seem well. Then they begin to cough. They cough until they bring up frothy pink fluid—their lungs drowning them from within. We give them oxygen when we have it. We give them morphine when the end is near. We can do nothing else. I have held the hands of men who drowned in their own lungs. I have felt them stop breathing. I have closed their eyes and moved to the next bed because there is always a next bed.

**Amputations**: Dr. Duhamel—the chief surgeon, a kind man, exhausted beyond measure—performs five, six, eight amputations a day. Arms, legs, sometimes both legs. He moves quickly because speed saves lives. He tells me that in peacetime, he would take two hours for such a surgery. Here he takes twenty minutes. The men scream even through the chloroform. I hold them down. I have become very strong.

**Faces**: There are men here who have no faces. Not metaphorically, Claire. *Literally*. Their jaws are gone, their noses, their eyes. They are alive but they are not recognizable as human. We feed them through tubes. We bandage them so the other patients cannot see. Some of them beg us to let them die. We are not allowed to.

---

I have learned things I never wanted to know:

I have learned that a man can live for hours with his intestines exposed. I have learned that morphine does not always work. I have learned the smell of gangrene—sweet and rotten, like fruit left too long in the sun. I have learned that you can become accustomed to anything, even horror, and that is perhaps the most terrible thing of all.

I have learned that I can look at suffering and feel nothing. Not pity. Not grief. Just a distant, professional assessment: *This one will survive. This one will not. This one requires immediate surgery. This one is beyond help.*

This frightens me. What have I become?

---

There are small mercies, rare and precious:

Yesterday a boy—eighteen, perhaps, from Marseille—died in my arms. He had been asking for his mother for two days. At the end, he looked at me and called me *Maman*. I did not correct him. I stroked his hair and told him I was there, that he was safe, that I loved him. He died believing his mother had come. I am glad I lied.

There is a soldier in Ward B who has lost both legs. He should be in despair, but instead he makes jokes. He calls himself "the best dancer in the hospital—all feet and no legs." He makes the other men laugh. I do not know where he finds the strength for joy. I think he is the bravest person I have ever met.

The surgeons and doctors work until they collapse. Dr. Duhamel operated for fourteen hours yesterday without rest. When he was done, he sat on the floor of the operating theater and wept. Not from weakness—from the sheer weight of what he had seen. Then he stood up, washed his hands, and returned to the ward. This is courage.

---

Claire, you asked me in your last letter if I regretted volunteering. You asked if I wished I had stayed in Lyon with you and Papa.

No.

I do not regret it. These men need care, and I can give it. That is enough. It has to be enough.

But I will tell you this: I am not the same person who left Lyon in February. That girl—the one who wanted to serve France, who wanted to be brave, who thought war was something noble—she is gone. In her place is someone harder, colder, more frightened.

I do not sleep well. When I close my eyes I see the faces of the men I could not save. I hear their voices calling for water, for mercy, for God. I wake in the night and for a moment I think I am still in the ward, and I must remind myself: *You are in your cot. You are not on duty. You are allowed to rest.*

But the rest never feels like rest.

---

Papa wrote to me last week. He said he was proud of me. He said I was doing my duty to France.

I do not care about France, Claire. I am not here for France. I am here for the boy from Marseille who died thinking his mother held him. I am here for the man with no legs who makes jokes. I am here for the surgeon who weeps and then returns to work.

I am here because someone must be, and I am able.

---

The fighting continues. We hear the guns even here, seventy-five kilometers from the front. They sound like distant thunder, constant and low. More wounded arrive every day. Dr. Duhamel says the offensive shows no sign of ending. He says we must prepare for summer, for months more of this.

I do not know if I can bear months more of this.

But I will. Because there is no choice.

---

Claire, I must stop. My two hours are almost gone, and I am due back in the ward. Please do not worry for me. I am safe—safer than the men I tend. I eat regularly (when I remember). I am in no danger from shells or bullets.

I am only in danger of forgetting who I was.

Kiss Papa for me. Tell him I am well. Tell him I am proud to serve. (These are lies, but they are kind lies, and he needs them.)

Write to me when you can. Tell me about Lyon. Tell me about ordinary things—the weather, the market, the neighbor's cat. I need to remember that the world still contains such things.

I love you. I will come home. I do not know when, but I will come home, and we will sit together in the garden and drink tea and I will try to forget.

But I do not think I will ever forget.

Your sister,
Marie

P.S.—If you send a package, send chocolate and cigarettes. Not for me—for the men. Chocolate is better than morphine for morale, and cigarettes are a currency more valuable than francs.

---

**Historical Note**: This letter is a composite based on actual French nurse correspondence from Verdun 1916. Female nurses (infirmières) were volunteers from the Red Cross, religious orders, or civilian organizations. They were not officially part of the military but worked in field hospitals under military authority. Conditions described (overcrowding, 18-hour shifts, types of wounds) are accurate to period medical reports. The emotional toll on medical personnel was widely documented but rarely discussed publicly during the war. Many nurses suffered what would now be diagnosed as PTSD. Marie Dubois is a fictional character, but her experiences reflect those of hundreds of French nurses who served at Verdun. Letters like this were less censored than soldiers' letters, as they did not contain tactical information.
