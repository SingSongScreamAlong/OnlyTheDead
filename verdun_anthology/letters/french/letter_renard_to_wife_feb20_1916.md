# LETTER: Caporal Marcel Renard to Wife
**From**: Cpl. Marcel Renard, 59th Chasseurs à Pied
**To**: Mme. Amélie Renard, Lille (occupied territory - letter never sent)
**Date**: February 20, 1916
**Location**: Bois des Caures, Verdun Sector
**Status**: Found in Renard's effects after his death (June 4, 1916)
**Condition**: Mud-stained, never mailed (no postal service to occupied Lille)

---

My dearest Amélie,

I do not know if you will ever read this. The Germans hold Lille still, and no letter I write reaches you. But I write anyway, because to write is to believe you are there, listening, and that belief keeps me whole.

It has been nearly two years since I saw your face. Two years since I held Céleste. She was five then. She is seven now, if she lives. God, let her live. Let you both live.

I am in a forest. Or what was a forest. The trees here have no branches anymore, just stumps, like broken teeth pointing at the sky. We have been here three weeks, waiting. For what, we do not know. The officers say nothing is coming. But I am not an officer. I am a worker from Lille, and I know when something is wrong the way a man knows when a machine is about to break. There is a feeling in the air, a weight, like a storm coming.

The men are quiet. Even the young ones, the boys who came in January, are quiet now. They feel it too.

I think of you at night when I cannot sleep. I think of the apartment on rue de Béthune, the kitchen with the yellow curtains you made, the way Céleste would sit on my lap while I read the newspaper and pretend to read along. I think of your hands, the way you kneaded bread, the way you touched my face when I came home from the factory.

I wonder if the Germans have taken the apartment. I wonder if you are still there, or if they have moved you somewhere else. I wonder if Céleste remembers me. Two years is a long time for a child.

I carry her ribbon. The blue one. You remember—she gave it to me the morning I left, when she cried and said, "Papa, don't go." I said, "I must, little one. But I will come back." I do not know if I lied.

The ribbon is filthy now, gray with mud, but I carry it in my left pocket, over my heart. When I am afraid, I touch it. It helps.

I want you to know that I am not a coward. I do not run. I do my duty. When the time comes—and I think it will come soon—I will do what must be done. Not for France. Not for the Republic. Not for any general or flag.

For you. For Céleste.

So that one day, if we win, if this madness ends, you will be free again. So that Céleste will grow up in a Lille without Germans in the streets. So that she will have a father she can be proud of, or at least a father who tried.

There is a boy here, nineteen years old, from Paris. Beaumont is his name. Red hair, freckles. Terrified. He asked me today if I thought he would die. I told him no. I do not know if I lied about that, either.

I do not know if I will die. I do not think about it. Or I try not to. But tonight, with the silence pressing down, I think about it.

If I die, Amélie, know this:

I loved you. I loved Céleste. I thought of you every day. You were the reason I did not go mad. You were the reason I kept going.

And if I do not die—if God or luck or simple chance brings me through—I will come home. I will find you, wherever you are. I will bring Céleste a new ribbon, a clean one, blue like the sky. And I will never leave again.

The candle is burning low. I must sleep. Tomorrow may be my last chance.

Kiss Céleste for me. Tell her Papa loves her.

Forever,
Marcel
