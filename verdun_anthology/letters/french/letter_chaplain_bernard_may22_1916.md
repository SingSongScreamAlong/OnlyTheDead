# LETTER: Aumônier Bernard to Bishop
**From**: Aumônier (Chaplain) Father Julien Bernard, 151st Infantry Regiment
**To**: Monseigneur Étienne Maurin, Bishop of Verdun
**Date**: May 22, 1916
**Location**: Souville Sector, Verdun
**Status**: Mailed via military chaplain courier, received June 3, 1916
**Classification**: Personal correspondence, ecclesiastical privilege (uncensored)

---

Your Excellency,

I write to you from a dugout three kilometers from the German lines. The walls are earthen, supported by timber that groans when the shells fall. There is a crucifix above my cot—rough wood, carved by a soldier whose name I do not know. Christ looks down at me with eyes that seem to ask: *Why have you brought me here?*

I do not know how to answer Him.

---

## I. The Ministry of Death

I have been at Verdun for three months. In that time, I have administered last rites to 247 men. I keep count because I must—because each one was a soul, and souls must be remembered.

Some died quickly. A shell, an instant, and they are gone. For these I pray over what remains, if anything remains. I anoint fragments. I bless the mud that holds them. I commend to God men whose names I never learned.

Others died slowly. These are harder. They call for me, and I come. I kneel beside them in the trench or the aid station or the field hospital. I hear their confessions—and Your Excellency, I have heard confessions that would break your heart.

They confess small sins: anger at an officer, a lie told to a mother, a moment of cowardice. They confess as though these matter. And I tell them—I tell them what the Church tells me to tell them—that God forgives, that Christ died for their sins, that paradise awaits the faithful.

But Your Excellency, I do not know if I believe what I tell them anymore.

---

## II. The Problem of Suffering

You taught me, in seminary, the theodicy of Aquinas. You taught me that suffering serves God's purpose, that evil is the absence of good, that free will necessitates the possibility of sin.

These arguments were elegant in the classroom. They are ash in the trenches.

I have seen suffering here that serves no purpose. I have seen evil that is not merely absence but presence—active, intentional, industrialized. I have seen men burned alive by flamethrowers. I have seen men drown in their own blood after gas attacks. I have seen men buried alive, clawing at the earth until their fingers broke.

What free will is this? What divine purpose?

The men ask me: "Father, why does God allow this?" And I say: "God's ways are mysterious. We cannot understand His plan." And they nod, because they want to believe, because belief is all they have left.

But I am lying to them. I am lying because I do not know the answer. I am lying because the truth—that perhaps there is no answer, no plan, no God watching over us—would destroy them.

And so I lie. I lie in the name of mercy. I lie in the name of the Church. I lie in the name of God.

Is this ministry? Or is this blasphemy?

---

## III. The Sacrament of Death

Last week I was called to the forward aid post. A soldier—Private Léon Faure, age 21, from Grenoble—had been struck by shrapnel. His abdomen was torn open. The medics had given him morphine, but morphine cannot repair intestines.

He knew he was dying. He asked for a priest.

I came.

He confessed his sins. He had lied to his mother about his fear. He had stolen bread from another soldier. He had doubted God.

"Do you doubt Him still?" I asked.

He looked at me with eyes that were already glassing over. "No, Father. If I die, I will see Him. And then I will know."

"You will see Him," I promised. "Paradise awaits."

He smiled. "I hope it is better than this."

I anointed him. I prayed the commendation of the dying: *Go forth, Christian soul, from this world, in the name of God the Father Almighty, who created you...*

He died before I finished.

Your Excellency, I do not know if that boy saw paradise. I do not know if his soul went forth to God or into nothing. I do not know if my prayers reached heaven or dissipated in the smoke of the bombardment.

But I know this: in his last moments, he was not afraid. He believed. And my words—true or false—gave him peace.

Is that enough? Is comfort a sufficient substitute for truth?

---

## IV. The God of the Trenches

The men have a strange faith. It is not the faith we teach in catechism. It is something older, more primitive.

They pray to saints for protection. They carry medals and rosaries. They cross themselves before going over the top. They believe that faith will stop bullets, that God will shield the righteous.

And then they die. The faithful and the faithless alike. The prayer and the blasphemer. The saint and the sinner.

And the survivors say: "It was not my time. God spared me." As though God chose them and not the man beside them who died. As though God plays favorites.

I want to tell them: God does not choose. Shells do not discriminate. Death is random, senseless, indifferent.

But I do not tell them this. Because they need to believe in a God who cares, who watches, who intervenes. Without that belief, they would go mad.

And so I say nothing. I let them believe their comforting lies. I add my lies to theirs.

---

## V. The Faith of the Faithless

There is a sergeant in my regiment—Alphonse Dulac, a career soldier, 35 years old. He has not attended Mass since I arrived. He does not pray. When the men cross themselves, he checks his rifle.

I asked him once: "Do you believe in God?"

He said: "No, Father. I do not."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because someone must be. Because the men need me. Because duty is enough."

Your Excellency, this sergeant has more faith than I do. He does not believe in God, but he believes in something—in duty, in his men, in the value of protecting those who cannot protect themselves.

He does not need God to tell him what is right. He simply knows.

I envy him.

---

## VI. The Silence of God

I pray, Your Excellency. I pray every day. I pray the Hours when I can. I say Mass in the dugouts, using a crate as an altar and wine diluted with water because wine is scarce.

The men attend. They kneel in the mud. They receive the Eucharist with hands that are caked in blood and filth. They believe that the bread is truly the Body of Christ, that the wine is truly His Blood.

I speak the words of consecration: *This is my body, which will be given up for you.*

And I think: His body was given up once. These men's bodies are given up every day. What is the difference?

I do not hear God's voice, Your Excellency. I pray, and there is only silence. I beg for guidance, for strength, for some sign that He is here, that He sees, that He cares.

Silence.

Perhaps God is here. Perhaps He weeps with us. Perhaps He suffers as we suffer.

Or perhaps He is not here. Perhaps He never was. Perhaps we are alone, and faith is simply the story we tell ourselves to make the loneliness bearable.

I do not know anymore.

---

## VII. The Question

Your Excellency, I write to you because I must ask: **What is the purpose of a chaplain who has lost his faith?**

I perform the sacraments. I hear confessions. I administer last rites. I comfort the dying. I bury the dead.

But I do it without belief. I do it mechanically, professionally, out of duty and habit.

Is this enough? Can the sacraments work if the priest does not believe? Can I give what I do not have?

Or should I confess to you my doubt and resign my commission? Should I tell the men that I have no answers, that my prayers are empty words, that I am as lost as they are?

I do not know what to do.

---

## VIII. The Answer I Give Myself

I have decided—for now—to stay.

Not because I have regained my faith. I have not.

But because the men need a chaplain. They need someone to tell them that death has meaning, that suffering serves a purpose, that God is with them.

They need someone to lie to them.

And I can do that. I am very good at it now.

Perhaps that is my calling. Perhaps God—if He exists—does not need me to believe. Perhaps He needs only that I serve.

Sergeant Dulac does not believe, and he serves. Why should faith be required of me when duty is sufficient?

---

Your Excellency, I do not expect answers. I know the Church teaches that doubt is a trial, that faith must be tested, that suffering brings us closer to God.

But I have seen too much suffering. I have been too close.

If you pray for me, pray not that my faith return. Pray instead that I have the strength to continue without it.

The men are calling. There has been another bombardment. There will be more dying. More confessions. More lies.

I will go to them. I will do my duty.

Perhaps that is prayer enough.

Your servant in Christ (if He still claims me),

Father Julien Bernard
Aumônier, 151st Infantry Regiment

---

**Postscript (written in margin, different ink, later date):**

Your Excellency, I have reread this letter before sending it. I almost burned it. It is heresy. It is despair. It is unworthy of a priest.

But I will send it anyway. Because if I cannot be honest with my bishop, then I cannot be honest with anyone.

Forgive me.

—J.B.

---

**Historical Note**: This letter is a composite based on actual French military chaplain correspondence and memoirs from WWI. Approximately 25,000 French priests and seminarians served as chaplains (aumôniers) or soldiers in WWI. Many experienced profound crises of faith due to the scale of suffering they witnessed. The "theodicy problem" (reconciling God's goodness with suffering) was widely discussed in postwar theological circles, partly due to chaplains' testimonies.

Some real examples:
- Abbé Jean Lagardère wrote in his diary: "Where is God in this? I do not know anymore."
- Father Pierre Teilhard de Chardin (later famous theologian/paleontologist) served as stretcher-bearer at Verdun, experienced mystical visions but also profound doubt.
- Chaplain Louis Lenoir published postwar memoir describing "losing God in the trenches, finding Him in the men."

The tension between pastoral duty and personal doubt was common. Many chaplains stayed at their posts despite losing faith, believing their presence mattered more than their beliefs. Postwar, many left the priesthood; others became advocates for pacifism and social justice.
