# THE DEATH OF MORT-HOMME
## Complete Battle Narrative: March 6 - May 20, 1916

**Location**: Le Mort-Homme (Hill 295) and Hill 304, Left Bank of the Meuse
**Period**: March 6 - May 20, 1916 (76 days of continuous battle)
**French Defenders**: 69th Division, 67th Division, 34th Division (rotated)
**German Attackers**: Bavarian Reserve Corps, XV Corps, VI Reserve Corps
**Result**: German tactical victory (summits captured) but strategic stalemate

**Casualties (Estimated)**:
- French: ~89,000 (killed, wounded, missing)
- German: ~78,000
- Total: ~167,000 casualties for two hills

---

## PROLOGUE: THE DEAD MAN'S HILL

The hill had carried the name for centuries—**Le Mort-Homme**, "The Dead Man"—long before 1916 gave it new meaning.

Local legend claimed a burial mound crowned its summit. Roman, perhaps, or Gallic. No one knew. The hill rose 295 meters above sea level, unremarkable in height, covered in scrub oak and bramble. Farmers avoided it. The soil was poor. The slopes were steep. It had no value.

Until February 21, 1916, when the Germans attacked Verdun and discovered that the hill's western slopes provided enfilading fire into their advance.

Then Le Mort-Homme became the most valuable piece of ground in France.

And 167,000 men would die proving it.

## PART I: THE ASSAULT BEGINS

### March 6, 1916 — 07:00

**Sous-Lieutenant Étienne Blanchard, 69th Infantry Division**

The bombardment that morning was different.

Blanchard had endured two weeks of shelling on the Right Bank before his division was rotated to the Left Bank. He thought he had learned to distinguish between routine harassment fire and preparation barrages.

This was neither.

This was methodical.

The German guns—he counted the distinct calibers: 77mm, 105mm, 150mm, 210mm—worked in sequence, starting at the summit of Mort-Homme and walking down the slopes in a grid pattern. Each section of trench received exactly five minutes of fire before the guns shifted 50 meters and repeated.

It was not the fury of February 21. It was the patience of a surgeon.

At 09:00, the bombardment stopped.

The silence was worse than the noise.

Blanchard stood at the firing step of his command trench, binoculars raised. Through the smoke he saw them: gray-green waves emerging from the German trenches 400 meters distant.

"*Stand to!*" he shouted.

His men—60 infantry, 1 machine-gun section—moved to the parapet. They were veterans of the Right Bank. They did not panic. They loaded rifles with the automatic motions of factory workers.

The Germans came on in textbook formation: skirmish line first, stormtroopers with grenades; main body 50 meters behind; reserves 100 meters back.

Blanchard waited until 200 meters.

"*Fire!*"

The machine gun opened first—*tak-tak-tak-tak*—scything through the skirmish line. Rifles joined in ragged volleys. Germans fell. Others came on. Some threw themselves flat and returned fire.

At 100 meters, German grenades began landing in the trench. One detonated near the machine gun; the gunner screamed, clutching his face. Another man took his place.

At 50 meters, the German stormtroopers rushed the trench.

Close-quarters combat is not heroic. It is butchery.

Blanchard shot a German in the chest at five meters. The man fell backward, eyes wide. Another German appeared, bayonet extended. Blanchard parried with his rifle, the blades scraping, and drove his own bayonet into the man's stomach. The German gasped—not a scream, just surprise—and collapsed.

A grenade landed at Blanchard's feet.

He dove.

The explosion lifted him, slammed him into the trench wall. His ears rang. Blood ran from his nose. He could not hear.

When his vision cleared, he saw Germans in the trench, fighting his men hand-to-hand. A French corporal swung his rifle like a club, caving in a German's skull. A German drove his bayonet through a French private's throat.

Blanchard found his pistol, fired three times. One German fell.

Then French reinforcements arrived—a platoon from the second line—and the Germans withdrew, dragging their wounded.

The attack had lasted 20 minutes.

Blanchard counted his casualties: 14 dead, 23 wounded.

The Germans would attack again at 14:00.

And again at 16:00.

And again the next day.

And the day after that.

## PART II: THE GRINDING

### March 10, 1916 — Night

**Caporal Louis Vernet, 69th Division**

They stopped counting the attacks after the fifth day.

Vernet's section—originally 12 men—was down to 7. Two killed outright, two evacuated wounded, one missing (buried when a shell collapsed the trench). Replacements arrived daily but rarely survived long enough for Vernet to learn their names.

Tonight was resupply night.

The communication trench that connected the front line to the rear was no longer passable during daylight. German artillery interdiction was too accurate. So supplies came at night: ammunition, rations, water, sandbags, trench mortar bombs.

And the dead went back the other way.

Vernet's section had been assigned burial detail. Three bodies from this morning's attack lay at the trench's base, wrapped in tent canvas. They had been there since 11:00. Rigor mortis had set in. The bodies were stiff, awkward to carry.

"Grab his legs," Vernet said to a young replacement—**Privat**, maybe, or **Prudhomme**, he could not remember.

They lifted the first body. It was heavier than Vernet expected. Death adds weight somehow.

They carried it down the communication trench, stumbling over debris: broken duckboards, spent shell casings, a boot (no foot inside, thank God). German flares periodically lit the sky. When the light came, Vernet and the replacement froze, hoping the Germans would not see movement.

Machine guns traversed the trench at irregular intervals.

*Tak-tak-tak.*

Bullets snapped overhead.

They kept moving.

At the rear trench, a burial party waited. They took the body without comment, added it to a pile of others. Vernet counted 30 bodies. This was one night's collection.

"How many more?" the burial sergeant asked.

"Two," Vernet said.

"Bring them. We leave in 20 minutes."

Vernet and the replacement returned to the front line, retrieved the second body. Then the third.

By the time they finished, Vernet's uniform was soaked with sweat and something else—fluids leaking from the corpses. The smell clung to him. Soap would not remove it. He knew from experience.

Back in the front-line trench, Vernet opened a tin of *singe* (canned monkey meat, the soldiers called it, though the label said beef). He ate mechanically. Taste did not matter. Eating was maintenance, like oiling a rifle.

The replacement sat beside him, trembling.

"First time on burial detail?" Vernet asked.

The boy nodded. He was 19, maybe 20. His hands shook.

"You get used to it," Vernet said.

That was a lie. But it was a kind lie.

### March 14, 1916 — Hill 304

**Soldat Henri Moreau, 56th Chasseurs (attached to 67th Division)**

[*Excerpt from Moreau's diary*]

We were sent to Hill 304 yesterday. They say it is a secondary position. They say the real battle is on Mort-Homme. They say Hill 304 will be quiet.

They were wrong.

The Germans attacked at dawn. Not with infantry—with artillery. I have never seen so many shells in so small a space.

Hill 304 is smaller than Mort-Homme. The summit is perhaps 200 meters across. The Germans put 200 guns on it this morning. One gun per meter.

The hill ceased to exist. It became smoke and fire. Trees were uprooted. Trenches collapsed. The earth itself seemed to boil.

I sheltered in a dugout with 12 others. We pressed ourselves against the walls. The beams above us groaned with each impact. Dust fell in streams. A shell landed directly above us—we felt the pressure in our chests, the air sucked from the dugout—but the beams held.

Someone prayed. Someone wept. I did neither. I simply waited.

At 10:00, the shelling stopped.

We emerged.

The landscape was unrecognizable. The trees were gone. The trenches were filled with dirt. Where there had been a command post, there was now a crater 10 meters wide.

Bodies—pieces of bodies—lay everywhere. An arm here. A leg there. A torso without a head. I stepped over a man's face, severed cleanly, eyes still open.

The Germans attacked at 10:30.

We repulsed them. Barely.

They attacked again at 14:00.

Again at 17:00.

We are still here. For now.

But Hill 304 is no longer a hill. It is a grave that has not yet been filled.

## PART III: THE SALIENT

### March 20, 1916 — Gas Attack

**Sous-Lieutenant Blanchard**

The gas came at dawn.

Blanchard woke to shouting: "*Gaz! Gaz! Masques!*"

He fumbled for his gas mask—the M2, clumsy canvas and rubber with fogged eyepieces—and pulled it over his head. The straps tangled in his hair. He tightened them, sealed the mask against his face, and breathed.

The air tasted of rubber and his own sweat.

He looked over the parapet.

A wall of yellow-green mist rolled across no-man's-land like fog, but fog does not cling to the ground like this. Fog does not move with purpose.

Phosgene. Or chlorine. Perhaps both.

The gas reached the trench.

Most of the men had their masks on. Some fumbled with straps. One man—a replacement who had arrived yesterday—could not find his mask. He tore through his pack, panicking.

"*Hold your breath!*" Blanchard shouted, his voice muffled by the mask.

The man tried. His face turned red, then purple. He gasped, inhaled.

The gas entered his lungs.

He screamed—a wet, choking scream—and collapsed. His body convulsed. Foam bubbled from his mouth. His eyes bulged.

Blanchard grabbed him, tried to drag him to the aid station, but the man's lungs were already drowning in their own fluids. He died in 30 seconds.

The gas lingered for 10 minutes, filling the trench, seeping into dugouts. When it finally dissipated, Blanchard counted casualties: 8 dead from gas, 14 incapacitated (lungs seared, unable to fight).

The German infantry attacked through the gas at 06:00.

They wore masks. They were prepared. This was coordinated.

Blanchard's section repulsed the attack but lost 11 more men.

By 08:00, Blanchard commanded 23 men. He had started the battle with 60.

## PART IV: THE ROTATION

### April 2, 1916 — Relief

**Caporal Vernet**

After 27 days on Mort-Homme, the 69th Division was rotated off the line.

Vernet's section marched down the communication trench in single file, rifles slung, packs heavy with personal belongings (not much: a spare shirt, letters from home, a rosary for those who still believed).

They passed the relieving unit—the 34th Division—marching up. Fresh troops. Clean uniforms. Eyes that had not yet seen what Mort-Homme does to men.

One of the new men asked Vernet: "What's it like up there?"

Vernet looked at him. The boy was 20, maybe younger. He had not shaved. His helmet was too large for his head.

"It's Hell," Vernet said. "But Hell is better than this."

The boy looked confused.

Vernet kept walking.

---

### April 5, 1916 — Rear Area, Verdun

**Sous-Lieutenant Blanchard**

The division was bivouacked 15 kilometers behind the line. Far enough that the guns were a distant rumble. Close enough that they never stopped.

Blanchard stood in line at the field kitchen, tin cup in hand, waiting for coffee. The line moved slowly. No one spoke. Conversation required energy they did not have.

When his turn came, the cook ladled coffee into Blanchard's cup. It was not coffee—it was chicory and burned grain—but it was hot. Blanchard drank it, felt the warmth spread through his chest.

He found a spot beneath a tree, sat, closed his eyes.

For the first time in 30 days, he was not responsible for anything. No men to command. No trench to hold. No Germans to kill.

He could rest.

But when he closed his eyes, he saw the trench. He heard the shells. He smelled the gas.

He opened his eyes.

A chaplain walked past, offering blessings. Blanchard did not call out to him.

He had stopped believing in God on March 10.

## PART V: THE RETURN

### April 9, 1916 — French Counterattack

**Capitaine Antoine Roche, 69th Division (returned to line)**

General Nivelle had ordered a counterattack.

Objective: Retake the Mort-Homme summit, lost to the Germans in late March.

Method: *Barrage roulant*—rolling barrage. Nivelle's innovation. Artillery advances in timed increments; infantry follows 50 meters behind the barrage.

In theory, the infantry reaches the German trenches while the defenders are still sheltering from artillery.

In practice, things are more complicated.

Roche stood in the jumping-off trench, checking his watch. 04:20. Ten minutes until the barrage began.

His company—120 men (understrength)—crouched in the trench, waiting. Some prayed. Some smoked. Some stared at nothing.

Roche had briefed them: "Stay behind the barrage. Fifty meters, no closer, no farther. When the barrage lifts, rush the trench. Do not stop. Do not hesitate. Speed is survival."

At 04:30, the French guns opened fire.

Two hundred seventy-five-millimeter guns, firing in unison. The sound was not sound—it was pressure, shaking the earth, rattling teeth.

The shells landed 200 meters ahead, on the German front line. Explosions erupted in sequence: one, two, three, a continuous chain of fire and smoke.

"*En avant!*" Roche shouted.

The company climbed out of the trench, formed a line, advanced.

The ground was churned mud, pocked with craters. Roche's boots sank ankle-deep with each step. He kept his eyes on the barrage, 50 meters ahead.

The barrage advanced 100 meters every five minutes, precise as a metronome. Roche's company followed.

At 04:40, the barrage lifted from the German front line, shifted to the second line.

Roche broke into a run. His men followed.

They reached the German trench at 04:42.

The Germans were there. They had not been suppressed. They had sheltered in deep dugouts and emerged when the barrage lifted.

Roche saw a German machine gun traversing toward him. He threw himself flat. The burst passed overhead.

His men poured into the trench, fighting hand-to-hand. Bayonets. Grenades. Rifle butts. Screaming.

Roche stood, pistol drawn, shot a German soldier who appeared from a dugout entrance. The man fell backward, clutching his chest.

The fighting lasted 10 minutes.

When it ended, the trench was French.

But Roche counted his casualties: 34 dead, 48 wounded.

He had gained 200 meters of trench.

At noon, the Germans counterattacked and retook it.

## PART VI: MAY — THE FINAL PUSH

### May 3, 1916 — Hill 304 Falls

**Soldat Moreau**

[*Diary entry*]

Hill 304 fell today.

We held for two months. We endured bombardments that turned the hill into a moonscape. We repulsed attacks until we ran out of ammunition and threw rocks.

But today the Germans came with flamethrowers.

I have heard of flamethrowers. I have never seen one. Now I wish I had not.

The German assault troops carried tanks on their backs—fuel tanks—and hoses that sprayed fire. Not flame from a match. Liquid fire. Fire that clings to skin and cloth and burns even when you roll in the mud to smother it.

The first wave of flamethrowers reached our trench at 10:00. The flames filled the trench, turned it into a furnace. Men burned alive, screaming. Those who did not burn ran. Those who ran were shot by German infantry following behind the flamethrowers.

I was in a reserve trench 200 meters back. I watched the front line collapse.

By 11:00, the Germans held the summit.

We counterattacked at 14:00. We failed. Three thousand casualties in two hours.

Hill 304 is German now.

The men say that if 304 falls, Mort-Homme will fall next.

I believe them.

### May 20, 1916 — Mort-Homme Falls

**Sous-Lieutenant Blanchard**

It ended the way it began: with a bombardment.

But this time, the Germans did not stop after five minutes per sector. They fired for six hours. Straight. Without pause.

Blanchard's trench ceased to be a trench. It became a series of disconnected craters. The walls collapsed. The parapets disintegrated. The dugouts—those that had not already caved in—were buried.

Blanchard and four men sheltered in a shell hole, helmets pressed to the ground, hands over ears.

The shells landed in waves: HE first, cratering the ground; shrapnel next, scything anything above ground; gas last, filling the craters with yellow-green death.

At 10:00, the shelling stopped.

Blanchard raised his head.

The world was silent. No birds. No wind. Just ringing in his ears.

He looked around. Of the four men who had sheltered with him, two were dead—killed by shrapnel despite being below ground level. The other two stared at nothing, eyes vacant. Shell shock. They would not fight today.

Blanchard stood, alone.

The German infantry appeared 100 meters away. A wall of gray-green. Hundreds of them. Maybe thousands.

Blanchard raised his rifle, fired. The rifle jammed—mud in the breech. He drew his pistol, fired six times. He did not know if he hit anyone.

The Germans kept coming.

Blanchard stood his ground. Not from courage. From exhaustion. He simply had no energy left to run.

A German stormtrooper appeared in front of him, rifle raised.

Blanchard waited for the bullet.

It did not come.

The German lowered his rifle, gestured toward the rear with his bayonet: *Go.*

Blanchard went.

He walked down the eastern slope of Mort-Homme, past burning ammunition dumps, past aid stations overflowing with wounded, past officers shouting orders that no one followed.

At 12:30, the Germans raised their flag on the summit of Mort-Homme.

Blanchard watched from 500 meters away.

The battle was over.

## EPILOGUE: THE COST

### May 21, 1916 — Casualty Count

**Major Édouard Lemaire, 69th Division, Medical Officer**

Lemaire sat in the divisional headquarters dugout, compiling casualty reports.

The 69th Division had been rotated on and off Mort-Homme three times between March 6 and May 20. Each rotation: two weeks on the line, one week in reserve.

The numbers:

- **March 6 deployment**: 15,000 men
- **May 21 strength**: 6,200 men
- **Casualties**: 8,800 (60% casualty rate)

Breakdown:
- Killed or died of wounds: 2,400
- Wounded (evacuated): 4,100
- Missing (buried, captured, deserted): 1,800
- Shell shock (incapacitated): 500

The missing troubled Lemaire most. Where were they? Buried under collapsed trenches, blown to pieces too small to identify, wandering in shock behind German lines?

He would never know.

He signed the report, sealed it in an envelope, sent it up the chain of command.

The report would be filed, summarized, and forgotten.

But the 8,800 men would not come back.

---

### May 22, 1916 — Reflection

**Sous-Lieutenant Blanchard**

Blanchard sat in a field hospital tent, bandages around his hands (burns from a shell fragment, minor).

A nurse brought him soup. He ate slowly, mechanically.

A chaplain approached. "Would you like to talk, Lieutenant?"

Blanchard looked at him. "About what?"

"God. Faith. Meaning."

Blanchard laughed—a hollow, brittle sound. "There is no meaning. We held a hill for 76 days and lost 9,000 men. The Germans lost 8,000 taking it. They gained 295 meters of elevation. Was it worth 17,000 lives?"

The chaplain hesitated. "I cannot answer that."

"No," Blanchard said. "You cannot."

The chaplain walked away.

Blanchard finished his soup, lay down on the cot, closed his eyes.

He dreamed of Mort-Homme.

He always dreamed of Mort-Homme.

## HISTORICAL POSTSCRIPT

**Mort-Homme After May 20, 1916**:
- Germans held summit until October 1916
- French recaptured summit in October counteroffensive
- Hill changed hands 14 times during 1916
- Post-war: Hill designated *Zone Rouge* (red zone, contaminated)
- Human remains still discovered today during excavation

**Commemoration**:
- Monument erected 1922: "They did not pass" (*Ils ne sont pas passés*)
- Ossuary contains remains of 10,000+ unidentified soldiers
- Annual ceremonies held each May 20 (anniversary of fall)

**Legacy**:
- Mort-Homme epitomizes Verdun's futility and heroism
- Symbol of attritional warfare's human cost
- Name gains second meaning: "The Dead Man's Hill" becomes literal

## GAME INTEGRATION

### Mission Design Opportunities

**Campaign: "The Death of Mort-Homme" (5-mission arc)**

**Mission 1: "March 6 — The Storm Begins"**
- Player: Blanchard (French officer)
- Objective: Survive first German assault
- Mechanic: Defensive combat, squad command, trench fighting
- Ends with nightfall; player realizes this is just the beginning

**Mission 2: "The Grinding"**
- Player: Vernet (French corporal)
- Objective: Complete burial detail, resupply under fire, hold line
- Mechanic: Survival horror elements (nighttime, flares, random shelling)
- Focus on atmosphere and tension, not heroics

**Mission 3: "Gas Attack"**
- Player: Blanchard
- Objective: Survive gas attack, repulse German assault
- Mechanic: Gas mask management (limited visibility, time limit before mask fails)
- Some NPCs die to gas (player cannot save everyone)

**Mission 4: "April 9 — The Counterattack"**
- Player: Roche (French officer)
- Objective: Lead counterattack using rolling barrage
- Mechanic: Stay behind barrage (too close = death, too far = lose barrage protection)
- Mission "succeeds" (trench captured) but feels hollow (massive casualties, Germans retake by evening)

**Mission 5: "May 20 — The Fall"**
- Player: Blanchard
- Objective: Hold Mort-Homme summit against final German push
- Mechanic: Overwhelming odds, player knows position will fall
- Ends with player's withdrawal (not death); survival is the victory

### Thematic Integration

**Futility**: Mort-Homme changes hands repeatedly; neither side "wins"
**Endurance**: French soldiers hold far longer than seems possible
**Cost**: 167,000 casualties for one hill; game asks "Was it worth it?" (no answer given)
**Memory**: Post-mission codex entries show Mort-Homme today (ossuary, monuments)

### Visual Design

**Environment Evolution**: Show hill degrading over missions
- Mission 1: Trees, trenches intact
- Mission 3: Trees destroyed, trenches cratered
- Mission 5: No vegetation, moonscape, craters upon craters

**Audio Design**:
- Constant background rumble of artillery (never silent)
- Gas attack: Muffled sound through mask, labored breathing
- Flamethrower: Horrific roar, screams
