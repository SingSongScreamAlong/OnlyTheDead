## Historical Context
By October 1916, German forces had held Fort Douaumont for 242 days. The French were preparing a massive offensive to recapture it. German soldiers knew the assault was coming. This diary represents their perspective during the final defense.

## Diary Entries - October 1916

### Oktober 10, 1916 - Freitag

The French artillery has increased. Every night now, shells fall on our positions. Not the wild drumfire of February - they have learned patience. But every shell reminds us they are preparing something.

We have worked for weeks reinforcing Douaumont. The fort is a fortress again, but it is also a tomb. Three levels of corridors, all dark, all smelling of mold and death. Men sleep in shifts because there is not enough space. The air is thick. When the shells hit, you cannot breathe.

Oberst Müller says the French are concentrating Moroccan Colonial troops across from us. Everyone knows what that means. The Senegalese and Algerians are fierce fighters. They say the Black soldiers show no mercy. But then, do we?

I have been at Verdun since March. Seven months in Hell. I no longer remember what silence sounds like.

---

### Oktober 20, 1916 - Freitag

The bombardment has begun in earnest. For four days now - day and night - the French artillery pounds us. It is worse than anything we did to them in February. Someone with a dark sense of humor calculated: if the February bombardment was 1 million shells, the French are repaying with interest.

We stay deep in the fort. The surface is death. Men who venture out for sentry duty often do not return. Yesterday, a shell collapsed an entire section of trench. Eight men buried alive. We can hear them sometimes, screaming beneath the rubble. But we cannot dig - the shells never stop.

Water is running low. The cistern was damaged three days ago. Rations are short. But that matters little. If the bombardment continues, we will go mad before we starve.

I think of my wife Greta. Does she still wait for me in München? Does she still believe I will return? I have stopped believing.

---

### Oktober 23, 1916 - Montag

The bombardment lifted this morning. For ten minutes, there was silence. Blessed, terrifying silence.

Then we heard them coming. The Trommelfeuer had stopped because their infantry was advancing. We manned the positions - those of us who could still walk after four days of bombardment.

They came in waves. Hundreds of them. The Colonials in the lead - tall men in different uniforms, shouting in languages I do not recognize. Behind them, French infantry in horizon blue, endless lines of them.

We fired until our barrels glowed red. The machine guns cut them down in rows, but still they came. For every man who fell, two more took his place. Where do the French find so many men?

They reached the wire. We threw grenades. They threw grenades back. The smoke was so thick I could not see ten meters. Men fought hand-to-hand in the smoke. I clubbed a French soldier with my rifle. He fell. I do not know if I killed him. I do not want to know.

By noon they had pulled back. We held. But we lost 40 men. We started this morning with 120 defending the fort. Now 80. And they will attack again tomorrow.

---

### Oktober 24, 1916 - Dienstag - 10:00 Uhr

They are coming again. The bombardment resumed at dawn - even heavier than before. Oberst Müller is dead. A shell hit the command post. Leadership has collapsed. Each section fights alone now.

I am writing this because I do not think I will survive to write again. If this diary is found by French soldiers, I ask that you send it to:

> Greta Schmidt
> Leopoldstraße 47
> München, Germany

Tell her I died fighting. Tell her I loved her to the last. Tell her not to remember me as I became here, but as I was when I left - young and hopeful and believing the war would be short.

Tell her I am sorry.

---

### Oktober 24, 1916 - Dienstag - 14:30 Uhr

*[This entry is in different handwriting, barely legible]*

Schmidt is dead. Shot through the chest at 13:00 when the Colonials breached the fort. He died quickly. He did not suffer long.

The fort has fallen. We are surrendering. There are 40 of us left from 250. The French Colonial officer who accepted our surrender treated us with dignity. He said we fought well. Small comfort.

I am taking Schmidt's diary. I will try to get it to his wife if I survive captivity.

The battle is over. The French have their fort back. What did any of us win? Only the dead have won. They at least have found peace.

— *Unteroffizier Paul Becker*
