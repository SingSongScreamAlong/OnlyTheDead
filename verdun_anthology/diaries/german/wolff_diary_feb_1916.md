# TAGEBUCH VON GEFREITER HANS WOLFF
## (DIARY OF PRIVATE FIRST CLASS HANS WOLFF)
### 24th Brandenburg Regiment, 3rd Division
### February 1916

**Owner**: Gefreiter Hans-Joachim Wolff (DE011)
**Period**: February 12 - February 28, 1916
**Location**: Verdun Offensive
**Physical Description**: Small black notebook, good condition, methodical handwriting
**Current Location**: Bundesarchiv-Militärarchiv, Freiburg (BA-MA MSg 2/3847)
**Language**: German (translated)
**Note**: Wolff was present at the capture of Fort Douaumont, February 25, 1916

---

## 12. Februar 1916 — Azannes
**Abends** (Evening)

We have been moved into position for a week. Something large is coming. No one tells us what, but the evidence is everywhere:

Ammunition dumps stretching for kilometers. Guns—so many guns. I counted sixty heavy howitzers just along our sector, and we are only one regiment. Engineers laying telephone cables. Supply trains arriving day and night.

The men speculate. Some say we are going to Verdun. Others say this is a feint and we will attack elsewhere. Officers say nothing.

I am a bank clerk from Berlin. I am not a soldier by nature or inclination. But I follow orders. That is all one can do.

The weather is cold. It has been snowing. They say this has delayed the operation.

I am not eager for the operation to begin.

---

## 20. Februar 1916
**Nachmittag** (Afternoon)

Orders came today. Tomorrow morning, 07:15 hours, the artillery bombardment begins. Infantry assault at 16:00 hours.

Officers briefed us: Nine hours of bombardment. One million shells. "Nothing will survive."

I believe them. I have seen the guns.

Our objective: Bois des Caures. A forest held by French chasseurs. After the bombardment, we are told, there will be no resistance. We will simply walk through and consolidate.

The men are nervous. So am I. I have been in combat before (Marne, 1914), but not like this. This is something new. Something monstrous.

I wrote a letter to Greta today. I did not post it. I do not know what to say.

If I die tomorrow, who will manage the accounts at the bank? Who will feed the cat?

These are the thoughts a man has before battle. Not glory. Not the Fatherland. Cats and accounts.

---

## 21. Februar 1916
**07:15**

It has begun.

I am writing this in the dugout. The noise is indescribable. Even here, 400 meters behind the guns, the earth shakes. The walls vibrate. Dust falls from the ceiling.

I cannot imagine what it is like in the French trenches.

I pity them. They are the enemy, yes, but they are also men. And no man deserves what we are doing to them.

---

**16:30**

We went over at 16:00.

The barrage lifted. We climbed out of the trenches and advanced through the snow.

The French positions... there is nothing left. The forest is splinters. The trenches are craters. The smell—burned wood, explosives, something sweet and wrong.

Bodies. Parts of bodies. Buried in the mud.

Some Frenchmen were still alive. They came out of dugouts, stunned, deafened, hands raised. We took them prisoner. One of them—a boy, maybe nineteen—wept. He kept saying something I could not understand. Maybe he was praying.

We advanced 2 kilometers before nightfall. Resistance was light. Most of the chasseurs are dead or fled.

I did not fire my rifle. There was no one to fire at.

This is modern war. The artillery kills. Infantry just occupies.

---

## 22. Februar 1916
**Morgen** (Morning)

We are in what was Bois des Caures. I use past tense because it is not a forest anymore. It is a graveyard.

Last night was cold. We slept in captured French dugouts. They are better than ours—deeper, more solid. The French build well.

This morning we found the body of a French colonel. Driant, his name was. Papers identified him. He was shot in the head, apparently leading a counterattack.

Our officers ordered him buried with honors. A chaplain said words over the grave. Some of the men saluted.

I do not know why we do this—honor a dead enemy. Perhaps because he was brave. Perhaps because we are not yet beasts.

Not yet.

---

## 23. Februar 1916
**Evening**

We pushed forward again today. Beaumont village taken. French are withdrawing toward Fort Douaumont.

Casualties have been light for us. Heavy for the French. I saw columns of prisoners being marched to the rear. Hundreds. They look dazed. Broken.

I feel no triumph. Only relief that I am alive.

Hans Müller from my platoon was killed this afternoon. Sniper. He was standing in the wrong place at the wrong time. One bullet. He was alive, and then he was not.

We buried him in a shell hole. No chaplain. No words. We did not have time.

This is how men disappear.

---

## 24. Februar 1916
**Night**

We are within sight of Fort Douaumont. It sits on the ridge like a fortress from a medieval tale. Enormous. Concrete and steel. Everyone says it is impregnable.

Tomorrow we attack it.

I do not want to attack it. I want to go home. I want to sit in my office at the bank and add columns of numbers and worry about whether Herr Schneider's account is overdrawn.

But I will attack it. Because that is what soldiers do.

Greta, if you ever read this: I am sorry. I tried. I do not want to die in France. But if I do, know that I thought of you at the end.

---

## 25. Februar 1916 — Abends (Evening)
**THE CAPTURE OF FORT DOUAUMONT**

I do not know how to write this.

We captured Fort Douaumont today.

Not "we" as in our division. "We" as in ten men from my company.

This morning, Lt. Radtke led a reconnaissance patrol toward the fort. I was ordered to accompany him. We approached cautiously, expecting machine-gun fire, artillery, minefields.

Nothing.

The fort appeared empty. Radtke ordered us forward. We climbed through the wire (undefended!), crossed the moat (no fire!), found an entrance.

Still nothing.

We entered. The fort was *occupied*—but by only a handful of old men. Territorials. Fifty, maybe sixty years old. They surrendered immediately.

Lt. Radtke took formal possession. He ordered me to plant a flag on the parapet.

And that was it. Fort Douaumont, the strongest fortification in the world, taken by ten men without firing a shot.

Later in the day, more troops arrived. Commanders arrived. Photographers arrived.

Lt. Radtke is being hailed as a hero. There is talk of decorations. The Iron Cross, first class.

I do not feel like a hero. I feel like a man who walked into an empty room and was awarded a medal for it.

But Fort Douaumont is ours. The French must be in shock.

---

## 26. Februar 1916
**Afternoon**

The French are counterattacking. Heavy shelling all day. They want Douaumont back.

We are inside the fort now, sheltering from their artillery. It is strange: yesterday this was a French fort. Today it is ours. Tomorrow it may be theirs again.

The fort is vast. Tunnels and galleries and chambers. It smells of mildew and urine and something else—cordite, maybe, or old explosives.

Outside, the battle continues. Inside, we wait.

I am alive. I do not know for how long.

---

## 28. Februar 1916
**Night**

One week since the offensive began. We have advanced 6 kilometers. Thousands of French dead. Thousands of prisoners.

But the advance has slowed. The French are reinforcing. Their artillery is increasing. We are taking casualties now.

This morning, a shell landed near our position. Three men killed instantly. One of them was beside me. One moment he was there, complaining about the cold. The next moment he was gone. Just pieces.

I vomited. I am not ashamed to write it. I vomited and then I sat down and shook for ten minutes.

This is what victory looks like.

---

**[Diary continues through June 1916; Wolff survived Verdun and the war]**

---

**Archivist's Note**: Hans-Joachim Wolff's diary is one of several German firsthand accounts of the capture of Fort Douaumont. His account corroborates other testimonies: the fort was taken by a small patrol led by Lt. Eugen Radtke (though credit for the capture was disputed between Radtke and Sgt. Kunze). Wolff's matter-of-fact tone and admission of fear provide a humanizing counterpoint to German propaganda accounts. After the war, Wolff returned to his bank in Berlin, worked there until retirement, and died in 1962. His daughter donated the diary to the Bundesarchiv in 1985.

**Historical Significance**: This diary provides German perspective on:
- Pre-assault preparations and morale
- The February 21 bombardment (from German side)
- The accidental capture of Douaumont (key event)
- German casualties and psychology
- Contrast to French accounts (e.g., Moreau)
