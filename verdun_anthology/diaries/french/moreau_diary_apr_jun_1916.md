# DIARY OF SOUS-LIEUTENANT HENRI MOREAU
## Continuation: April - June 1916

**Owner**: Sous-Lt. Henri Moreau (FR002)
**Period**: April 1 - June 30, 1916
**Location**: Mort-Homme, Thiaumont, Fleury sectors
**Condition**: Increasingly deteriorated; bloodstains, water damage, some pages torn out

---

## April 5, 1916 — Mort-Homme
**Afternoon**

Back in the line. Ten days of "rest" in a village that shakes every time the guns fire, and now we are back.

Mort-Homme. Dead Man's Hill. I understand the name now.

We have been fighting for this hill since March 6. One month. The Germans hold the northern slopes. We hold the summit and southern slopes. Between us: 500 meters of cratered mud and the dead.

We attack. They attack. The hill changes hands. Or rather, parts of the hill change hands. One trench to another. One shell hole to another.

It never ends.

I asked Sergeant Dulac today: "Why does Mort-Homme matter? It's just a hill. There are other hills."

He looked at me and said: "It matters because we say it matters. And because they say it matters. And because hundreds of men have died for it, so it must matter, or else they died for nothing."

"Maybe they did die for nothing," I said.

Dulac said nothing. He just cleaned his rifle and looked away.

---

## April 9, 1916
**Evening**

We counterattacked today. At dawn. Nivelle's orders: push the Germans off the northern slopes.

We went over at 04:30. Rolling barrage—Nivelle's specialty. The artillery walked forward in timed increments, and we followed behind.

It almost worked.

We reached the German trenches. Hand-to-hand combat. I shot two men. One of them was very young. Younger than Beaumont.

But the Germans brought up reserves. Machine guns. We were forced back.

We gained 200 meters. We lost 150 men.

Was this victory? Pétain's order was read to us afterward: "Magnificent attack. You have shown the enemy that France does not yield."

The men listened in silence. No one cheered.

---

## April 22, 1916
**Night**

I have not written in two weeks. There has been nothing to say.

The fighting continues. The dying continues. I am still alive. That is all.

Today something happened. Small, meaningless, but I write it down because I must write something or I will forget that there are things worth writing.

A bird. A lark, I think. It landed on the parapet while we were under bombardment. Just sat there, as though shells were merely weather.

One of the men—Privat, the pigeon keeper from Vaux—looked at it and said: "The birds don't care about the war. They just sing."

And we listened. And for maybe thirty seconds, we heard the bird singing, and the shells were just noise in the background.

Then the bird flew away.

Privat said: "I wish I was a bird."

We all wished we were birds.

---

## May 1, 1916
**Morning**

We have been pulled off Mort-Homme. Transferred to the right bank. Thiaumont sector.

Thiaumont is worse than Mort-Homme. If that is possible.

There is a small fortification here—Ouvrage de Thiaumont. It has changed hands twelve times since April. Right now the French hold it. Tomorrow, who knows.

The Germans are pushing hard toward Fort Vaux. Thiaumont is on the approach. If we lose Thiaumont, Vaux is threatened.

So we hold Thiaumont.

I am so tired.

---

## May 20, 1916
**Afternoon**

News reached us: Mort-Homme has fallen.

The Germans took the summit two days ago. The hill we bled for since March 6—gone.

The men are quiet. Some are angry. Some just shrug. What does it matter? We took it, they took it, we took it again, now they have it. Tomorrow someone else will have it.

The hill does not care who owns it.

I think Dulac was right. We fight for Mort-Homme not because it matters strategically, but because we cannot admit it doesn't matter. Because if it doesn't matter, then all those deaths were meaningless.

And if those deaths were meaningless, then maybe all death here is meaningless.

I cannot think about this.

---

## May 24, 1916
**Night**

Thiaumont has fallen.

We held for four weeks. Today the Germans took it. Flamethrowers. Gas. Artillery. We could not hold.

We withdrew to the secondary line. Tomorrow we will counterattack and take it back. Then they will take it again.

This is Verdun.

---

## June 1, 1916
**Morning**

The Germans are attacking Fort Vaux. Massive bombardment. We can see the smoke from here.

Vaux is only two kilometers away. We are in reserve, waiting for orders.

Word is the fort is cut off. Surrounded. The garrison is fighting alone.

I wonder if Colonel Driant felt like this at Bois des Caures. Watching the bombardment. Knowing what was coming. Unable to escape.

I wonder if Major Raynal—commanding Vaux—knows he is about to die.

---

## June 4, 1916
**Evening**

A pigeon flew over our trenches today. Heading west, toward French lines. Someone shot at it—a German sniper, probably. Missed.

Later we heard: Fort Vaux sent a message. Last pigeon. Garrison is dying of thirst. No water for three days.

No relief force can reach them. The Germans hold all approaches.

Vaux is alone.

---

## June 7, 1916
**Night**

Fort Vaux has surrendered.

I did not believe it when I first heard. A French fort does not surrender. But Vaux did.

The story is filtering through. Seven days. No water. Men drinking from walls, drinking their own urine. Dying of thirst.

Major Raynal surrendered to save his men. The Germans let him keep his sword.

Is that defeat? Or is that something else?

I do not know anymore.

---

## June 12, 1916
**Afternoon**

We have been fighting for Thiaumont again. We took it back two days ago. Today the Germans took it again.

I have lost count. How many times has Thiaumont changed hands? Fifteen? Twenty?

It does not matter.

We fight for Thiaumont. They fight for Thiaumont. Men die for Thiaumont. Thiaumont remains a ruin.

There is no village anymore. Just craters. And the ouvrage—a concrete bunker, half-collapsed, filled with corpses.

We fight over corpses.

---

## June 19, 1916
**Morning**

Ahmed is dead.

Ahmed ben Mokhtar. Algerian tirailleur. Seven days ago he wrote me a letter—he was illiterate, I helped him write it for his brother. He asked me to mail it if he died.

Last night the Germans shelled our position with gas. Ahmed's mask was damaged. He breathed the gas. Phosgene.

He died this morning. Coughing up his lungs. There was nothing we could do.

I mailed his letter.

I do not know if his brother will ever receive it. Algiers is far away. And the French Army does not always deliver letters to colonial families.

But I mailed it anyway.

Because someone should remember Ahmed.

---

## June 23, 1916
**Evening**

The Germans have reached their farthest advance.

They are at Fleury—four kilometers from Verdun. If they break through here, the city falls.

We are being rushed forward. Every available man. This is the moment. This is the battle for Verdun itself.

I do not know if we can hold.

I have been at Verdun for four months. I have seen Driant die. I have seen Beaumont buried alive. I have seen Renard fall at Vaux. I have seen Ahmed choke on gas.

I have seen hundreds die. Thousands.

And now it is my turn. I know it. I feel it.

If you find this diary, tell my mother I was brave. (I was not. I was only still alive.)

Tell her I did my duty. (I did. What choice did I have?)

Tell her I thought of her at the end.

This is true.

---

## June 24, 1916
**Morning**

I am alive.

The Germans did not break through. They reached Fleury, and then... they stopped.

We do not know why. Perhaps they were exhausted. Perhaps they ran out of men. Perhaps God intervened. (I do not believe that. But some men do.)

We held Fleury. Barely.

The line holds.

For now.

---

## June 30, 1916
**Night**

June is over. Half a year since the war began. (No—half a year since Verdun began. The war started in 1914. I forget sometimes.)

I am still alive. I do not know why.

Dulac is alive. Privat is alive. Most of the others are dead.

We have been told that the British and French are launching a great offensive on the Somme. July 1. To relieve pressure on Verdun.

I do not know if it will work. But perhaps it does not matter. Verdun has become its own world. Its own war. It exists independently of strategies and offensives and generals' plans.

Verdun simply *is*. And we are in it. And we will stay in it until we die or the war ends.

I do not think the war will end.

I do not think anything ends.

We simply continue.

---

**[Diary continues through October 1916; final entries describe the French counteroffensive and recapture of Douaumont. Moreau survives.]**
