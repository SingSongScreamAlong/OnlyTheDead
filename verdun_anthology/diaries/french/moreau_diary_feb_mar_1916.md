# DIARY OF SOUS-LIEUTENANT HENRI MOREAU
## 56th Chasseurs à Pied / 151st Infantry Regiment
### February - March 1916

**Owner**: Sous-Lt. Henri Moreau (FR002)
**Period**: February 20 - March 31, 1916
**Location**: Verdun Sector
**Physical Description**: Small leather-bound notebook, water-damaged, mud-stained, blood on several pages
**Current Location**: Musée de la Grande Guerre, Meaux (donated by Moreau family, 1982)
**Condition**: Fragile; some entries illegible due to water damage

---

## February 20, 1916 — Bois des Caures
**07:00**

Clear morning. Cold. Colonel Driant inspected the positions at dawn. He is restless. He has been restless for days.

He gave me a letter to keep. For his wife. He says he has "a presentiment." I told him he must not speak this way. He smiled—sad, tired—and said, "I am too old to lie to myself, Henri."

The men sense something. Even Dulac, who fears nothing, checks his rifle three times. Renard sits in the trench, staring east. Beaumont is pale.

I do not know what is coming. But I feel it. Like standing on train tracks and feeling the vibration before you hear the engine.

God help us.

---

## February 21, 1916 — Bois des Caures
**07:15**

The world ended.

I do not know how else to write it. At 07:15 the guns began and the world ended.

I cannot describe the sound. It is not sound. It is the absence of everything else. It is pressure. It is hell made audible.

We are in the dugout. Six of us. The earth shakes. Timbers crack. Dust rains down. One of the men is praying. Another is weeping. I am writing this because if I do not write I will scream.

Beaumont is beside me. He is shaking. I put my hand on his shoulder. He looks at me with eyes that are not twenty years old. They are ancient. Terrified. I tell him we will survive. I do not believe it.

The shelling continues.

---

**11:00**

Still shelling. Four hours. The trench is gone. Everything is gone. We are in craters now.

Colonel Driant is everywhere. I do not know how he is alive. He moves from position to position, steadying men, giving orders. He is untouched. As though God has placed a hand over him.

Or God is saving him for something worse.

I am so tired. I have not slept. My ears are bleeding. I can barely hear. Everything is muffled, distant.

Beaumont asked me if we will die today. I said no. I lied.

---

**16:00**

They are coming.

The barrage lifted at noon. We thought it was over. We were fools.

At 16:00 the infantry came. Gray shapes through the smoke. Hundreds. Thousands.

We fired until the barrels burned our hands. We threw grenades. We fought with bayonets.

I killed a man. I shot him in the face at ten meters. He fell and did not move. I felt nothing.

Is this what war does? It makes you nothing?

---

## February 22, 1916 — Somewhere behind Bois des Caures
**06:00**

Bois des Caures is lost.

Colonel Driant is dead.

I saw it. He was at the ravine, covering our withdrawal. I begged him to come. He said, "Go, Henri. That is an order."

I went.

A shot. One shot. I turned and he was falling.

We could not recover his body. The Germans have it.

He was right. He knew. And I did not listen.

I am in the reserve trench now. Two hundred of us remain. There were two thousand yesterday.

Renard is here. Wounded, but alive. Dulac is here, cleaning his rifle as though nothing happened. Beaumont is not here.

I saw him fall. The trench collapsed and he disappeared into the mud. We could not reach him.

I told his mother he was not afraid. That was a lie.

I am covered in blood. Not mine. Someone else's. I do not know whose.

I have not slept in 36 hours.

The guns are starting again.

---

## February 25, 1916 — Bras-sur-Meuse (Reserve Position)
**18:00**

Fort Douaumont has fallen.

No one can believe it. The largest fort in the world, taken by a handful of Germans who simply walked in.

The garrison was fifty men. Fifty. Old men. Territorials.

Everyone is stunned. Joffre is reportedly furious. Generals are being sacked. Heads are rolling.

But Douaumont is gone.

I do not care about forts. I care that my men are dead. That Beaumont is dead. That Colonel Driant is dead.

The newspapers will write about Douaumont. They will not write about Beaumont. No one will remember him but his mother and me.

War is very good at forgetting.

---

## February 26, 1916
**Morning**

A new general has arrived. Pétain. I have heard of him. Defensive specialist. Methodical. Not loved, but respected.

He looks tired. Older than his years. But his eyes are steady.

He issued orders immediately: artillery reorganization, rotation of divisions, supply lines reinforced.

Someone said he told his staff, "They will not pass." *Ils ne passeront pas.*

I want to believe him.

---

## March 2, 1916 — Mort-Homme Sector (Transferred)
**Evening**

They have moved us to the left bank. Mort-Homme. "Dead Man's Hill." Appropriate name.

This place is worse than Bois des Caures. The shelling is constant. The mud is knee-deep. Men drown in shell holes.

I saw it today. A soldier slipped into a crater, weighted down by equipment. He thrashed. He screamed. Three of us tried to reach him. We could not. The mud was too deep.

He sank. It took five minutes. We watched.

After, we stood there in silence. What do you say? What do you do?

Nothing. You do nothing. You forget. Or you try to.

---

## March 8, 1916
**Night**

Beaumont is dead.

I knew it on February 21. But today it became official. A runner brought the casualty lists.

"Soldat Théodore Beaumont, 151e RI. Missing, presumed dead."

Missing. As though he might reappear. As though the mud might give him back.

His mother will receive a letter. Some officer—not me, I am too junior—will write: "Your son died bravely in the service of France." It will be a lie. He did not die bravely. He died terrified, crushed under tons of earth, calling for his mother.

But we do not write that. We write lies. Because the truth would break them.

I should write to her. I knew him. I could tell her something real. That he was kind. That he tried. That he wanted to go home.

But I will not write. Because I am a coward. Because I cannot bear to put his death into words again.

---

## March 15, 1916
**Afternoon**

The shelling continues. Day and night. We have become creatures of the underground.

I have not seen the sun in four days. I do not remember what it looks like.

My hands shake. I cannot stop them. Dulac says it is fatigue. Renard says nothing. He sits in the corner, holding his daughter's ribbon, staring at nothing.

We are all breaking. Some faster than others. But we are all breaking.

---

## March 20, 1916
**Morning**

Gas attack last night. Phosgene.

The masks saved most of us. But not all. Three men in our section did not get their masks on in time.

I heard them coughing. Wet, choking sounds. They drowned in their own lungs. It took hours.

We could do nothing.

This morning their bodies were gray. Lips blue. Eyes open. Frozen in the act of suffocation.

We buried them in a shell hole. No time for ceremony. No time for anything.

I am forgetting their names. I knew them for three weeks and I am already forgetting.

What kind of man forgets the dead?

---

## March 25, 1916
**Evening**

We were relieved today. Pulled out of the line after sixteen days.

Pétain's rotation system. The *Noria*, they call it. A water wheel. Endless, turning.

We are in a village ten kilometers back. There are beds. Hot food. A chance to wash.

I took off my uniform. It stood up by itself, caked with mud and blood and filth.

I looked at myself in a mirror—the first time in weeks. I did not recognize the face. Hollow eyes. Hollow cheeks. A stranger.

I am twenty-three years old. I look fifty.

---

## March 31, 1916
**Night**

Tomorrow we return to the line.

Eight days of rest. Not enough. Never enough.

But we go back. Because that is what soldiers do. We endure. We do not question. We do not refuse.

We go back into the mud and the shells and the death, and we do it again.

And again.

And again.

Until we die, or the war ends.

I do not know which will come first.

Colonel Driant told me once, "This war will break us all, Henri. The question is whether we break before it does."

I think I know the answer now.

---

---

**Archivist's Note**: This diary was preserved by Henri Moreau throughout the war. He survived Verdun and the war, though he was wounded twice more (Chemin des Dames, 1917; Marne, 1918). After the war he became a teacher in Lyon, married, had three children. He rarely spoke of Verdun. His daughter found this diary after his death in 1968 and donated it to the museum. It is one of the most complete French diaries from the Verdun battle. Certain passages have been quoted in numerous histories of Verdun (Horne, Ousby, Jankowski). The diary is available for research by appointment at the Musée de la Grande Guerre, Meaux.
