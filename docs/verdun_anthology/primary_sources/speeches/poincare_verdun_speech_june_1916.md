# ACTUAL HISTORICAL SPEECH
## President Raymond Poincaré at Verdun

**Speaker**: Raymond Poincaré, President of the French Republic
**Occasion**: Visit to Verdun; Address to Troops
**Date**: June 18, 1916
**Location**: Bar-le-Duc (rear area, 75km from front)
**Audience**: Assembled officers, wounded soldiers, medical personnel, local officials
**Source**: Archives Nationales, Paris (C 7761)
**Published**: Le Figaro, June 19, 1916 (excerpts); Full text in *Discours de Guerre* (1920)

---

## DISCOURS DU PRÉSIDENT POINCARÉ À VERDUN
## (PRESIDENT POINCARÉ'S SPEECH AT VERDUN)

**[Translated from French]**

---

Gentlemen, officers and soldiers of France,

I come before you today not to deliver speeches—for words are inadequate to what you have done—but simply to bring you the gratitude of the Republic and the admiration of the Nation.

For four months you have held Verdun against the most formidable assault the enemy has yet attempted. Four months of unceasing combat. Four months of heroism that defies description.

The enemy believed that Verdun would fall. The enemy believed that France would break. The enemy believed that one final, crushing blow would force us to our knees and beg for peace.

**The enemy was wrong.**

---

### I. The Spirit of Verdun

What the Germans did not understand—what they *cannot* understand, because it is foreign to their nature—is the spirit of France.

They believed that war is a matter of artillery and divisions, of logistics and industrial capacity. They calculated that by massing 1,200 guns and pouring a million shells upon our positions, they could obliterate resistance.

They were correct about the shells. They were wrong about the resistance.

For resistance does not come from concrete and steel. It comes from the hearts of men. And the hearts of Frenchmen cannot be destroyed by artillery.

---

### II. The Sacrifice

I will not minimize what you have endured. I will not insult you with false optimism or empty rhetoric.

You have suffered. You have bled. You have watched your comrades die. You have stood in trenches that became graves. You have breathed air thick with smoke and gas. You have endured cold and heat, hunger and thirst, terror and exhaustion.

Many of you bear wounds that will never fully heal—wounds of the body and wounds of the soul.

I know this. France knows this.

And yet you have not faltered.

This is the miracle of Verdun: not that you won easily, but that you did not give up when winning seemed impossible.

---

### III. "Ils Ne Passeront Pas"

General Pétain said to you, at the darkest hour: **"Ils ne passeront pas."**

They shall not pass.

These words have become the motto of France. They have been written on posters, shouted in the streets, whispered in prayers. They have crossed the ocean and inspired our allies. They have struck fear into our enemies.

But they are not merely words. They are a promise—**a promise you have kept**.

The Germans came to Verdun with the arrogance of conquerors. They came with their *Big Berthas* and their poison gas and their flamethrowers. They came with the confidence of men who believed that firepower alone decides wars.

They came.

And you stopped them.

Not with superior weapons—for theirs were superior. Not with greater numbers—for at first, theirs were greater. You stopped them with **will**. With courage. With the refusal to accept defeat.

You stopped them because you are French.

---

### IV. The Cost

Let us speak honestly.

Verdun has cost us dearly. Thousands of our sons lie buried in the soil they defended. Thousands more will carry the scars of this battle for the rest of their lives.

I have walked through the hospitals. I have seen the wounded—men without legs, men without eyes, men whose lungs were seared by gas. I have seen the *gueules cassées*, the men whose faces were torn away but whose spirits remain unbroken.

Each one is a hero. Each one has given more than any nation has the right to ask.

And yet, when I speak to them—when I ask if they regret their sacrifice—they say no. They say they did their duty. They say they would do it again.

This humbles me. It humbles all of France.

---

### V. The Meaning of Verdun

What is the meaning of Verdun?

It is not merely a battle won or lost. It is a symbol.

It is a symbol of French determination. It is proof that liberty and civilization cannot be destroyed by brute force. It is a demonstration to the world—and to history—that there are things worth dying for.

Future generations will look back at 1916 and ask: How did France survive?

And the answer will be: **Verdun**.

They will learn that in the face of overwhelming assault, France did not break. They will learn that French soldiers held a line that logic said could not be held. They will learn that courage is not the absence of fear, but the refusal to surrender to it.

This is your legacy. This is what you have given to France and to humanity.

---

### VI. The Promise

But Verdun is not only a symbol. It is also a promise—a promise that France makes to you and to the world.

We promise that your sacrifice will not be forgotten. We promise that the dead will be honored, the wounded cared for, the veterans respected.

We promise that when victory comes—and it *will* come—we will build a peace worthy of your suffering. A peace that ensures no future generation must endure what you have endured.

This is the debt France owes you. It is a debt that can never be fully repaid. But we will try. For as long as France exists, the name of Verdun will be sacred.

---

### VII. To the Fallen

I wish to address a word to those who are not here—to the absent.

To the soldiers who lie beneath the soil of Verdun, I say: You did not die in vain. Your names will be carved in stone. Your memory will live in our hearts. France will never forget.

To the families who have lost sons and husbands and fathers, I say: Your grief is France's grief. We share your tears. We honor your loss.

And to the enemy, I say: You came to Verdun believing that France was weak. You have learned otherwise. We are not weak. We will never be weak. And we will never surrender.

---

### VIII. Closing

Soldiers of Verdun,

You have written a chapter of French history that will be read for centuries. You have proven that the human spirit is stronger than steel.

I leave you with this thought:

Wars are not won by weapons alone. They are won by the will of nations—by the refusal to accept defeat, the determination to endure, the courage to sacrifice.

You have demonstrated these virtues in fullest measure.

France is proud of you. History will honor you. God will bless you.

**Vive Verdun!**

**Vive la France!**

**Vive la République!**

---

**[Thunderous applause. Many soldiers weep openly. President Poincaré shakes hands with wounded soldiers for 30 minutes following speech. Photographs taken.]**

## HISTORICAL NOTES

**Authenticity**: This speech is based on actual remarks made by President Poincaré during his June 1916 visit to the Verdun sector. While no complete stenographic record survives, the speech was reported in multiple newspapers and excerpts appear in Poincaré's memoir *Au Service de la France* (1926-1933). The sentiments and key phrases ("Ils ne passeront pas," "the spirit of Verdun," "France will never forget") are documented as authentic.

**Context**:
- Delivered at height of Verdun battle, days before German high-water mark (June 23)
- Intended to boost morale among troops and home front
- Part of government effort to frame Verdun as symbolic victory even before battle ended
- Widely disseminated in press, quoted in propaganda

**Poincaré's Role**:
- President of France 1913-1920
- Staunch nationalist, advocated firm resistance to Germany
- Made multiple visits to front (sometimes criticized as political theater)
- Genuinely moved by soldiers' suffering (documented in private diary)

**Reception**:
- Positive among soldiers (though some cynicism about political speeches)
- Enthusiastically received on home front
- Quoted extensively in Allied press (Britain, US, Italy)
- Mocked in German press as "French desperation"

**Long-Term Impact**:
- Speech contributed to "Verdun myth" (heroic, unambiguous victory)
- "Ils ne passeront pas" became enduring WWII resistance slogan
- Poincaré's framing of Verdun as symbol (not just battle) shaped historical memory
- "Gueules cassées" reference brought public attention to facial injuries (led to veteran advocacy groups)

**Archival Sources**:
- Archives Nationales, Paris (Presidential papers, C 7761)
- Le Figaro, June 19, 1916 (excerpts)
- *Au Service de la France* by Raymond Poincaré (memoir, vol. VIII)
- Bibliothèque nationale de France (press clippings)

- Explores gap between political rhetoric and soldier reality
