# MEDICAL PRACTICES & CONDITIONS
## Verdun 1916

---

## MEDICAL EVACUATION CHAIN

### French System

#### **1. Poste de Secours (Aid Post)**
- **Location**: Front-line trenches, dugouts, or shell holes
- **Staff**: 1-2 brancardiers (stretcher-bearers) per company, sometimes médecin auxiliaire (auxiliary doctor)
- **Treatment**:
  - Stop bleeding (tourniquets, pressure bandages)
  - Morphine injection (if available)
  - Triage: immediate evacuation vs. can wait vs. hopeless
- **Supplies**: Bandages, iodine, morphine, splints
- **Conditions**: Cramped, unsanitary, often under fire
- **Survival Rate**: Minimal surgery capability; primary goal is stabilize and evacuate

---

#### **2. Poste de Secours Régimentaire (Regimental Aid Station)**
- **Location**: 500m-1km behind front
- **Staff**: Médecin-major (regimental surgeon), 2-4 assistants
- **Treatment**:
  - Wound cleaning, debridement (removal of dead tissue)
  - Tetanus antitoxin injection
  - Amputation of obviously gangrenous limbs
  - Re-triage
- **Conditions**: Overwhelmed during attacks; hundreds of wounded in hours
- **Mortality**: High from shock, blood loss, infection

---

#### **3. Ambulance (Field Hospital)**
- **Location**: 5-10km behind lines (often in intact buildings, churches, barns)
- **Staff**: Surgical teams (chirurgiens), nurses, orderlies
- **Treatment**:
  - Emergency surgery (amputations, abdominal wounds, head trauma)
  - Blood transfusion (experimental in 1916; no blood typing yet)
  - Stabilization for transport
- **Capacity**: 200-400 beds (often exceeded)
- **Innovations**:
  - **Autochir (Autocirugical unit)**: Mobile surgical truck with operating theater, developed 1915
  - **X-ray units**: Mobile radiography for locating shrapnel

---

#### **4. Hôpital d'Évacuation (Evacuation Hospital)**
- **Location**: Rear areas (Bar-le-Duc, Nancy, Châlons)
- **Staff**: Full medical staff, specialist surgeons
- **Treatment**:
  - Reconstructive surgery
  - Treatment of infections
  - Long-term recovery
- **Conditions**: Better equipped but still primitive by modern standards

---

#### **5. Hôpital de l'Arrière (Base Hospital)**
- **Location**: Paris, Lyon, major cities
- **Staff**: Top surgeons, experimental treatments
- **Treatment**:
  - Facial reconstruction (pioneered by Dr. Hippolyte Morestin)
  - Prosthetics
  - Psychiatric care (shell shock)

---

### German System (Similar Structure)

#### **1. Truppenverbandplatz (Battalion Aid Post)**
- **Staff**: Sanitäter (medics)
- **Treatment**: First aid, triage

#### **2. Hauptverbandplatz (Main Dressing Station)**
- **Staff**: Stabsarzt (medical officer)
- **Treatment**: Surgery, stabilization

#### **3. Feldlazarett (Field Hospital)**
- **Location**: 10-20km rear
- **Treatment**: Major surgery, recovery

#### **4. Kriegslazarett (War Hospital)**
- **Location**: Germany (Cologne, Berlin, Munich)
- **Treatment**: Long-term care, rehabilitation

## COMMON INJURIES & TREATMENTS

### Shrapnel Wounds

#### **Description**
- Lead or steel fragments from shells, grenades, or bullets
- Range from superficial to deep organ damage
- Often multiple fragments in single casualty

#### **Treatment (1916)**
- **Debridement**: Removal of foreign material, dead tissue
- **Probing**: Manual or X-ray guided removal of fragments
- **Left In Situ**: Small fragments often left embedded (removal more dangerous than retention)
- **Infection Risk**: High; soil contamination common
- **Antibiotics**: None (not available until 1928 penicillin)

#### **Complications**
- **Gas Gangrene** (Clostridium infection): Rapidly fatal, requires immediate amputation
- **Tetanus**: Common; antitoxin partially effective if given early
- **Sepsis**: Often fatal

#### **Survival Rate**: ~60-70% if reached field hospital within 6 hours

---

### Amputations

#### **Indications**
- Severe trauma (limb destroyed)
- Gas gangrene (infection)
- Vascular damage (limb unsalvageable)

#### **Procedure (Field)**
- Anesthesia: Chloroform or ether (if available)
- Tourniquet applied
- Surgical saw used to cut bone
- Flaps of skin/muscle preserved to cover stump
- Cauterization or ligation of blood vessels
- **Duration**: 15-30 minutes (speed prioritized over precision)

#### **Mortality**
- **Upper limb**: 20-30%
- **Lower limb**: 30-40%
- **Bilateral (both legs)**: 60-70%

#### **Post-Op Complications**
- Infection (majority of deaths)
- Shock
- Phantom limb pain (no treatment)

#### **Prosthetics**
- Crude wooden/metal devices
- Issued months later (if at all)

---

### Gas Wounds (Phosgene, Chlorine, Mustard)

#### **Phosgene (Green Cross Shells)**
- **Mechanism**: Damages lung alveoli, causes pulmonary edema (fluid in lungs)
- **Symptoms**: Coughing, choking, frothy pink sputum, asphyxiation
- **Latency**: 24-48 hours (victim feels fine initially, then drowns internally)
- **Treatment (1916)**: None effective
  - Oxygen (limited availability)
  - Rest (exertion accelerates death)
  - Morphine for suffering
- **Survival**: Dependent on dose; heavy exposure = 80-90% fatal

#### **Chlorine (Early Gas, Less Common by 1916)**
- **Mechanism**: Lung irritant, immediate symptoms
- **Symptoms**: Burning eyes, coughing, suffocation
- **Treatment**: Fresh air, rest
- **Survival**: Better than phosgene if removed from gas quickly

#### **Blue Cross (Arsenic Irritant)**
- **Mechanism**: Induces vomiting, forces gas mask removal
- **Follow-Up**: Combined with lethal gas (Green Cross)
- **Treatment**: Symptomatic (anti-emetics ineffective)

#### **Long-Term Effects**
- Chronic respiratory damage
- Tuberculosis susceptibility
- Early death (1920s-1930s)

---

### Head Wounds

#### **Penetrating Skull Trauma**
- **Cause**: Bullets, shrapnel
- **Treatment**:
  - **Trepanation**: Drilling/cutting skull to remove fragments, relieve pressure
  - **Debridement**: Removal of bone fragments, foreign material
  - **Closure**: Scalp sutured over defect
- **Anesthesia**: Local (cocaine solution) or general (chloroform)
- **Mortality**: 50-70% (infection, brain damage)

#### **Non-Penetrating (Concussion)**
- **Symptoms**: Unconsciousness, confusion, headache
- **Treatment**: Rest, observation
- **Complications**: Intracranial bleeding (often fatal, no CT scans to detect)

#### **Facial Wounds**
- **Devastating**: Loss of jaw, nose, eyes
- **Treatment**:
  - Immediate: Stop bleeding, maintain airway
  - Long-term: Reconstructive surgery (pioneering work at Verdun)
    - **Dr. Hippolyte Morestin (France)**: Early plastic surgery, skin grafts
    - **Dr. Harold Gillies (Britain)**: Facial reconstruction techniques
- **Psychological**: Severe trauma; "Gueules Cassées" (Broken Faces) survivors faced social stigma

---

### Abdominal Wounds

#### **Challenge**: Penetration of intestines, liver, spleen
- **Mortality**: 80-90% (1916)
- **Treatment**:
  - Laparotomy (surgical opening of abdomen)
  - Repair of perforated organs
  - Resection of damaged bowel
  - **Problem**: Peritonitis (infection of abdominal cavity) almost always fatal without antibiotics

#### **Survival Factors**
- Speed of treatment (< 2 hours ideal)
- Limited contamination
- Luck

---

### Burns (Flamethrowers, Explosions)

#### **Types**
- **Thermal**: Fire, explosions
- **Chemical**: White phosphorus (grenades, shells)

#### **Treatment**:
- **Immediate**: Dousing with water, removal of clothing
- **Field**: Tannic acid solution (experimental, often worsened outcomes)
- **Hospital**: Debridement, dressings, skin grafts (limited success)
- **Pain Management**: Morphine

#### **Complications**
- Infection (universal)
- Scarring, contractures (permanent disfigurement)
- Psychological trauma

#### **Survival**: Dependent on extent; >40% body surface area = usually fatal

## ANESTHESIA

### Available Methods (1916)

#### **Chloroform**
- **Advantages**: Portable, effective
- **Disadvantages**: Cardiac toxicity, liver damage, narrow therapeutic window
- **Use**: Common in field hospitals

#### **Ether**
- **Advantages**: Safer than chloroform (wider margin)
- **Disadvantages**: Flammable, requires more equipment
- **Use**: Preferred when possible

#### **Local Anesthesia**
- **Cocaine Solution**: For minor procedures, dental, facial
- **Novocaine (Procaine)**: Introduced 1905, safer than cocaine

#### **None**
- **Reality**: Many field surgeries performed without anesthesia due to supply shortages
- **Method**: Restraint, bite block, speed
- **Trauma**: Extreme psychological and physical suffering

---

## PAIN MANAGEMENT

### **Morphine**
- **Form**: Injectable (sulfate solution), tablets
- **Availability**: Widespread (front-line medics carried syringes)
- **Dose**: 10-15mg standard (often repeated)
- **Addiction**: Recognized but considered secondary to immediate suffering

### **Alcohol**
- **Cognac, Rum, Wine**: Issued medicinally
- **Effect**: Minimal pain relief, some psychological comfort

### **Aspirin (Acetylsalicylic Acid)**
- **Use**: Mild pain, fever
- **Limitations**: Ineffective for severe trauma

---

## BLOOD TRANSFUSION (Experimental, 1916)

### **Challenges**
- **Blood Typing**: ABO system discovered 1901, but not standardized
- **Coagulation**: Blood clots rapidly (no anticoagulants yet)
- **Incompatibility**: Mismatched transfusions often fatal

### **Methods**
- **Direct Transfusion**: Donor to recipient, arm-to-arm (tubes connecting veins)
- **Stored Blood**: Not yet feasible (refrigeration, anticoagulants unavailable)

### **Pioneers**
- **Dr. Oswald Robertson (USA)**: Developed first blood bank (1917, late Verdun period)
- **French Experiments**: Limited trials at Verdun

### **Outcome**: Rare, risky, but sometimes life-saving

## INFECTION CONTROL

### **The Great Killer**: Infection (50-60% of wounded died from infection, not initial injury)

#### **Types**

##### **Gas Gangrene (Clostridium perfringens)**
- **Source**: Soil bacteria (Verdun soil heavily contaminated with manure, corpses)
- **Symptoms**: Swelling, blackening tissue, gas bubbles under skin, foul odor
- **Progression**: Rapid (hours to death)
- **Treatment**:
  - Immediate radical debridement (cut away all infected tissue)
  - Amputation (only effective treatment)
  - **Serum Therapy**: Experimental antitoxin (limited success)
- **Mortality**: 60-80% even with amputation

##### **Tetanus (Clostridium tetani)**
- **Symptoms**: Lockjaw, muscle spasms, respiratory failure
- **Prevention**: Antitoxin injection (developed 1890s, widely used by 1916)
- **Effectiveness**: Reduced tetanus deaths by ~90% (if given within 48 hours)

##### **Sepsis (Blood Poisoning)**
- **Cause**: Bacterial infection spreading to bloodstream
- **Symptoms**: Fever, chills, organ failure
- **Treatment (1916)**: None effective
  - Supportive care (fluids, rest)
  - Mortality: ~70-90%

#### **Prevention Attempts**
- **Iodine**: Topical disinfectant (some effectiveness)
- **Carbolic Acid (Phenol)**: Wound irrigation (toxic if absorbed)
- **Dakin's Solution**: Sodium hypochlorite (bleach) solution, developed 1915 by Alexis Carrel and Henry Dakin
  - **Method**: Continuous irrigation of wound via tubes
  - **Effectiveness**: Reduced infection rates significantly (30-40% reduction)
  - **Adoption**: Widespread by mid-1916

## SHELL SHOCK (Combat Stress Reaction)

### **Symptoms**
- **Physical**: Tremors, paralysis, mutism, blindness, deafness (no organic cause)
- **Psychological**: Nightmares, flashbacks, catatonia, violent outbursts, dissociation

### **Theories (1916)**
- **"Concussion Theory"**: Blast waves damage nervous system
- **"Emotional Collapse"**: Psychological breakdown from sustained stress
- **Debate**: Organic vs. psychological (unresolved in WWI)

### **Treatment**

#### **Frontline**
- **Rest**: 24-48 hours behind lines
- **"Pithiatism" (Dr. Jules Dejerine, France)**: Psychological persuasion, suggestion
- **Discipline**: Threat of court-martial for "cowardice"

#### **Hospitals**
- **Rest Cure**: Isolation, sedation
- **Faradization**: Electrical shocks (punitive "therapy")
- **Hypnosis**: Experimental
- **Occupational Therapy**: Crafts, manual labor

### **Prognosis**
- Mild cases: Returned to duty (often relapsed)
- Severe cases: Permanent invalidism
- **Long-Term**: Many suicides in 1920s-1930s (delayed trauma)

### **Controversy**
- Execution for desertion: Some shell-shock victims court-martialed and shot (UK executed 306, France fewer but uncounted)
- Post-war recognition: Slow; not fully understood until PTSD diagnosis (1980)

## DISEASE

### **Trench Fever**
- **Cause**: *Bartonella quintana* (bacterium spread by lice)
- **Symptoms**: High fever, headache, muscle pain, relapsing pattern
- **Treatment**: None (self-limiting, but debilitating for weeks)
- **Prevention**: Delousing (limited success)

### **Trench Foot**
- **Cause**: Prolonged exposure to cold, wet conditions
- **Symptoms**: Swelling, numbness, gangrene
- **Treatment**: Dry socks, foot powder, amputation if gangrenous
- **Prevention**: Whale oil, dry socks (difficult in Verdun mud)

### **Dysentery**
- **Cause**: Contaminated water, poor sanitation
- **Symptoms**: Bloody diarrhea, dehydration
- **Treatment**: Fluids, rest (antibiotics unavailable)
- **Mortality**: 5-10% (higher if combined with wounds)

### **Typhoid**
- **Cause**: *Salmonella typhi* (water/food contamination)
- **Prevention**: Vaccination (introduced pre-WWI, widely used)
- **Effectiveness**: Reduced typhoid deaths by ~90%

### **Influenza**
- **1916**: Seasonal flu, manageable
- **1918 Pandemic**: Not yet arrived (killed more than war itself)

## MEDICAL PERSONNEL

### **French**

#### **Médecins (Doctors)**
- **Total**: ~20,000 mobilized
- **Training**: Medical degree, often specialists (surgeons, internists)
- **Rank**: Officer (lieutenant to colonel)
- **Casualties**: ~2,000 killed/wounded (exposed to front-line danger)

#### **Infirmiers/Infirmières (Nurses)**
- **Male orderlies**: Enlisted soldiers, basic training
- **Female nurses**: Volunteers, Red Cross, religious orders
- **Restrictions**: Women not allowed in front-line aid posts (officially)
- **Famous**: Marie Curie (X-ray vehicles — "Petites Curies")

#### **Brancardiers (Stretcher-Bearers)**
- **Role**: Retrieve wounded under fire
- **Mortality**: ~15-20% (highest of medical personnel)
- **Heroism**: Often unarmed, Red Cross armband (not always respected)

### **German** (Similar Structure)
- **Sanitätsoffizier**: Medical officers
- **Sanitätssoldaten**: Medical orderlies
- **Krankenschwester**: Nurses

## MEDICAL INNOVATIONS AT VERDUN

### **French**
1. **Autochir (Mobile Surgery)**: Truck-mounted operating rooms, brought surgery closer to front
2. **Triage Systematization**: Rapid sorting of wounded (Immediate / Delayed / Expectant)
3. **Dakin-Carrel Method**: Antiseptic wound irrigation (saved thousands of limbs)
4. **Radiology**: Mobile X-ray units (Marie Curie's contribution)

### **German**
1. **Gas Warfare Medical Response**: Developed treatments for gas exposure (limited success)
2. **Blood Vessel Surgery**: Pioneering vascular repair techniques

## EVACUATION LOGISTICS

### **French (La Voie Sacrée)**
- **Ambulances**: 3,000+ motorized ambulances (converted trucks, buses)
- **Capacity**: 12-20 wounded per vehicle
- **Speed**: 5-10 km/h on damaged roads
- **Flow**: Continuous (one ambulance every 2-3 minutes)
- **Mortality During Transport**: ~5-10% (shock, bleeding)

### **Trains**
- **Hospital Trains**: Converted rail cars, equipped with beds, surgical equipment
- **Capacity**: 200-400 wounded
- **Route**: Verdun → Bar-le-Duc → Nancy → Paris

## PSYCHOLOGICAL IMPACT ON MEDICAL STAFF

### **Moral Injury**
- Doctors/nurses witnessed unrelenting suffering, often unable to save patients
- **Memoirs**: Many medical personnel wrote of feelings of helplessness, guilt

### **Breakdown**
- Some medical officers suffered breakdowns (classified as shell shock)
- **Suicide**: Rare but documented

### **Post-War**
- Many continued medical careers focused on trauma surgery, rehabilitation
- Development of veterans' hospitals, prosthetics industry

---

## LEGACY

### **Medical Advances**
- **Triage Systems**: Modern emergency medicine based on WWI triage
- **Plastic Surgery**: Facial reconstruction techniques pioneered at Verdun (Gillies, Morestin)
- **Blood Banks**: Robertson's work led to modern blood storage
- **Antisepsis**: Dakin-Carrel method influenced modern wound care

### **Psychological**
- **PTSD Recognition**: WWI shell shock eventually recognized as trauma disorder (1980)
- **Veterans' Care**: Established need for long-term psychological support

### **Cultural Memory**
- **"Gueules Cassées"**: Disfigured veterans became symbol of war's cost
- **Medical Memoirs**: Doctors' accounts (e.g., "Memoirs of a French Army Surgeon") shaped public memory
