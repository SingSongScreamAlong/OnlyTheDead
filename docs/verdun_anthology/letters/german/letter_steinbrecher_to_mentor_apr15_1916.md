# LETTER: Leutnant Friedrich Steinbrecher to Professor
**From**: Lt. Friedrich Steinbrecher, Reserve Infantry Regiment 76
**To**: Prof. Dr. Ernst Böhmer, University of Heidelberg, Theology Faculty
**Date**: April 15, 1916
**Location**: Verdun Sector (specific location censored)
**Status**: Mailed, received May 2, 1916
**Language**: German (translated)

---

Esteemed Professor Böhmer,

I write to you from a place that has no name in any theology I have studied.

It has been eighteen months since I left Heidelberg, eighteen months since we last sat in your study and discussed Aquinas and the problem of suffering. I remember arguing—with the confidence of youth and ignorance—that suffering must have meaning, that God permits evil only insofar as it serves a greater good, that the universe is rational.

I was a fool.

There is no meaning here. Or if there is, it is a meaning so alien to human comprehension that it might as well be meaninglessness.

You asked me, in your last letter, to describe what I see. I will try, though I fear the words will fail.

Imagine a landscape of craters. Not a few craters, scattered, but craters upon craters, overlapping, so that the earth has no flat surface anymore. Each crater is filled with water—gray water, the color of lead—and in the water are things I will not name. The air smells of decay and chemicals. The sky is perpetually gray with smoke. There are no trees, no grass, no birds. There is only mud and metal and the bodies of men.

We live underground, like rats. The dugouts are cold and wet and smell of excrement and gangrene. Men die of disease as often as they die of bullets. The lice are everywhere—in our clothes, our hair, our food. At night we hear the rats. They are fat, Professor. Fat on what the war provides.

The bombardments continue without pause. Sometimes for hours, sometimes for days. The sound is beyond description. It is not noise—it is pressure, it is physical force. It enters your skull and shakes your brain inside. Men go mad from it. I have seen them: eyes wide, mouths open, screaming silently because there is no sound left that is not the sound of shells.

Yesterday—or was it the day before? Time has no meaning here—a shell landed in the trench next to our dugout. I went to see if anyone survived. I will not describe what I found. I will only say that I stood there, looking at what remained, and I felt nothing. Not horror, not grief, not even disgust. Nothing.

This frightens me more than the shells.

I have become stone, Professor. I think we all have. The alternative is to become beasts, and I have seen men take that path too. Men who fight with knives and shovels and teeth, who kill with a joy that is demonic. Men who laugh when they kill.

I do not laugh. But I do not weep, either.

You will ask: Where is God in this?

I do not know.

I pray, still. The habit is too old to break. But I do not know to whom I pray. The God of mercy? He is not here. The God of justice? What justice is this? The God who sees the sparrow fall? He is blind, Professor, or he has turned his face away.

Perhaps there is no God. Perhaps there never was. Perhaps we invented Him because we could not bear the silence of the universe.

Or perhaps He exists, but He is not what we thought. Perhaps He is the God of Job—the God who speaks from the whirlwind and says, "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?" Perhaps He is beyond good and evil, beyond mercy and justice, beyond anything we can comprehend.

If so, then we are alone. More alone than we ever imagined.

I do not write this to blaspheme, Professor. I write it because I am lost. I am twenty-eight years old and I am lost in a way that has nothing to do with geography. I do not know who I am anymore. I do not know what I believe.

The man who sat in your study and debated theodicy is dead. He died somewhere between the Marne and here, and I do not know when.

What remains is this: a creature in field-gray who obeys orders, who ducks when shells fall, who eats when food is available, who sleeps when sleep is possible. A creature who survives because survival is instinct, not choice.

Is this what we are, Professor? Is this the image of God? Mud and fear and the stink of latrines?

Forgive me. I am not writing clearly. I have not slept in three days. The bombardment makes sleep impossible.

You asked if I would return to Heidelberg after the war, if I would resume my studies. I do not know if there will be an after. I do not know if I will survive. And if I do survive, I do not know what will survive of me.

But if I do return—if by some miracle I sit once more in your study—we will have much to discuss.

You wrote that you pray for me. Thank you. I do not know if it helps. But it cannot hurt.

Give my regards to the faculty. Tell them I remember the library, the bells of the Heiliggeistkirche, the view from the Philosophenweg. Tell them these things still exist, even if I cannot believe in them anymore.

I must close. We move to the line tonight.

With respect and affection,
Friedrich Steinbrecher
Leutnant, Reserve Infantry Regiment 76
