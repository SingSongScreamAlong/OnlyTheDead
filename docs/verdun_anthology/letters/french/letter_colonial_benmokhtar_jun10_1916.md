# LETTER: Caporal Ahmed ben Mokhtar to Brother
**From**: Cpl. Ahmed ben Mokhtar, 7th Régiment de Tirailleurs Algériens
**To**: Hassan ben Mokhtar, Algiers
**Date**: June 10, 1916
**Location**: Fleury Sector, Verdun
**Status**: Mailed via regimental postal service, received July 8, 1916 (delayed)
**Language**: French (colonial soldiers required to write in French; Arabic prohibited by censors)
**Censor Mark**: "PASSED / 7e RTA"

---

My brother Hassan,

I write to you in the language of *les Français* because that is the only language the censors permit. You will have someone read this to you, I know. Old Kader at the café reads French. Give him a few coins and he will read it aloud.

Brother, I am alive. That is the first thing you must know, the thing Mother and Father must know. I am alive, though many are not.

I have been at Verdun for four months. You asked me in your last letter—which reached me only yesterday, two months late—whether it is as terrible as the newspapers say.

The newspapers lie, Hassan. It is worse. Much worse.

---

## The Fighting

We fight in a place called Fleury. There used to be a village here. Now there is nothing—no houses, no church, no streets. Only craters and mud and the dead.

We attack the Germans. They attack us. The village changes hands. We take it, they take it back, we take it again. I have fought for Fleury six times. I do not know why Fleury matters. There is nothing there to take.

But we are told to take it, so we take it.

The Germans are good soldiers. They fight hard. They do not run easily. Their machine guns are very accurate. They kill many of us before we can get close.

But we are good soldiers too. The *tirailleurs* are feared by the Germans. They call us *schwarz Teufel*—black devils. (One of our officers speaks German and told us this.) They fear us because we fight with the knife and bayonet. We do not hesitate. This is our reputation.

I do not know if I am a devil, black or otherwise. I am a man from Algiers who does what he is told because he has no choice.

---

## The Cold and the Heat

When I arrived in February, it was cold. Colder than any winter in Algiers. My feet froze in my boots. We burned anything we could find for warmth—wood, coal, the furniture from destroyed houses.

The French gave us coats, but they are not made for men from Africa. We shivered while the French soldiers said, "This is nothing. You should see a real winter."

Now it is June and it is hot. The sun beats down on the mud and the smell of the dead rises. The French complain about the heat. We do not. This is a heat we know.

But the heat brings flies. Thousands, millions of flies. They cover the corpses. They crawl into our food. They land on our faces while we sleep. You cannot escape them.

The French say, "Even the flies have come to Verdun."

I say nothing. I think: The flies are smarter than us. They come for the dead. We come to die.

---

## The French

The French officers treat us differently than they treat French soldiers.

Some are kind. Our captain, Captain Moreau (not related to the lieutenant of the same name), speaks to us as men. He learns our names. He asks about our families. When we take a position, he praises us. When we fail, he does not curse us as dogs.

Others are not kind. They call us *bicots*, *ratons*—rats, monkeys. They say we are good for dying but not for thinking. They say we are savages who fight well because we are too stupid to be afraid.

I am not stupid, Hassan. I am afraid every day.

But I do not show it. Because if I show fear, they will say, "See? The Arab is a coward." And they will judge all of us by me.

So I am brave. Or I pretend to be brave. Is there a difference?

---

## The Moroccan Brothers

There are Moroccan troops here—*tirailleurs marocains*. They are our brothers in faith and in service. We pray together when we can. Five times a day, if the shelling permits. Often it does not.

The French watch us pray. Some are curious. Some are respectful. Some laugh and say, "What good does prayer do here?"

I do not answer. But I think: Prayer reminds me that I am a man, not a beast. Prayer reminds me that there is something beyond this mud and death. Prayer reminds me that God is merciful, even if men are not.

One of the Moroccans—his name is Youssef, from Marrakech—was killed two days ago. Shrapnel to the chest. He died quickly, *alhamdulillah*.

We buried him facing Mecca. Captain Moreau allowed this. He even stood with us while we prayed the *Salat al-Janazah* (the funeral prayer). He did not understand the words, but he bowed his head.

This is why I respect Captain Moreau. He sees us as men, not tools.

---

## The Question

You asked me in your letter why I fight for France. You said that France has taken our land, taxed our people, treated us as subjects not citizens. You said, "Why should you die for them?"

Hassan, you are right. France has done all these things.

But I fight anyway.

Not because I love France. I do not. I fight because I was conscripted. I fight because if I refuse, they will shoot me or imprison me, and our family will suffer.

I fight because I have no choice.

But also—and this is harder to explain—I fight because my brothers fight. The *tirailleurs*, the Moroccans, even the French soldiers who treat us fairly. We fight together. We die together. And when you stand beside a man in battle, when you see him wounded and you carry him back, when he shares his water with you even though water is scarce—then he becomes your brother, no matter what flag he serves.

I do not fight for France. I fight for the man beside me.

Does that make sense? I do not know if I am explaining it well.

---

## The Promises

The officers tell us that after the war, things will be different. They say that Algeria will have more rights, more representation. They say that those of us who fight will be honored as equals.

I do not believe them.

But some of the *tirailleurs* do. They believe that bleeding for France will earn them citizenship, respect, equality. They believe that France will remember what we did here.

I hope they are right. But I have lived long enough to know that powerful men forget their promises when it is convenient.

Still, we hope. Because hope is all we have.

---

## The Dead

Many *tirailleurs* have died at Verdun. I do not know how many. Hundreds, certainly. Maybe thousands.

They are buried here, in the mud of France. Far from home. Far from Mecca.

I think about this often. If I die here, Hassan, my body will lie in French soil forever. You will never see my grave. Mother will never visit it. I will be forgotten, a name on a list, one of many colonial soldiers who died for a country that was not theirs.

This bothers me more than death itself.

If I die, Hassan, you must tell Mother that I died as a Muslim. That I prayed. That I did not abandon my faith even when I fought for those who do not share it.

Tell her I thought of her. Tell her I thought of you, of Father, of home. Tell her I did not forget who I am.

---

## The Hope

I do not know when this war will end. The officers say it will be over soon. They have been saying this for two years.

I think it will not end soon. I think we will fight until there is no one left to fight.

But I survive. I am clever. I am fast. I keep my head down. I obey orders. I do not volunteer for dangerous missions. I do what I must and no more.

And I pray. To God, to Allah the Merciful, the Compassionate, I pray that He will bring me home.

I want to see Algiers again, Hassan. I want to walk through the *souk*. I want to hear the *muezzin* call the faithful to prayer from the mosques, not from a muddy trench in France. I want to drink mint tea and eat *couscous* that Mother makes, not the gray soup the French Army provides.

I want to go home.

If God wills it (*inshallah*), I will.

---

Hassan, tell Mother and Father that I am safe (a lie, but a kind one). Tell them I eat well (another lie). Tell them I will return (I hope this is not a lie, but I do not know).

Kiss Mother's hand for me. Tell Father I honor him by serving with discipline and courage, even if I do not serve by choice.

And you, little brother—if the French come to conscript you, run. Go to the desert. Go to the mountains. Do not let them take you. One son in this war is enough.

I must stop now. We move to the front line tonight. There will be another attack tomorrow.

Pray for me.

Your brother,
Ahmed

*Bismillah ar-Rahman ar-Rahim*
(In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful)

---

**Marginalia (in Arabic, censors did not catch it)**:
*Hassan—I am very afraid. Do not tell Mother. —A.*
