# THE GUNS BEGIN
## February 21, 1916 — Bois des Caures

---

## PART I: BEFORE

### 07:00 — The Last Quiet

Lieutenant-Colonel Émile Driant stood in the forward observation post, his breath misting in the cold morning air. Snow had fallen during the night—not much, just enough to dust the shattered stumps of the Bois des Caures with white. The forest was a graveyard of trees: broken trunks, skeletal branches, mud frozen into ridges where the autumn rains had carved trenches into gullies.

He was fifty-nine years old. Too old for this.

Through his field glasses, he scanned the German lines, 400 meters distant across the ravine. Nothing moved. The silence was complete. Not even the usual harassing fire that punctuated every dawn. It had been this way for two days now—this unnatural stillness.

Behind him, in the shallow dugout, Sous-Lieutenant Henri Moreau was writing in his diary by candlelight. Driant could hear the scratch of the pen, faint and steady. Twenty-three years old, that one. A lawyer in another life. Sharp mind. Good officer. The men trusted him.

"Moreau," Driant said, not turning from the parapet.

The scratching stopped. "Mon Colonel?"

"What time is it?"

"Seven o'clock, sir. Precisely."

Driant lowered the glasses. He looked to the east, where the sky was beginning to pale. The sun would rise soon, though it would bring no warmth. February in the Argonne—cold that entered the bones.

"They will come today," Driant said.

Moreau emerged from the dugout, buttoning his coat. His face was thin, intelligent, marked by two years of war. There was a scar on his left temple from the Marne—a shell fragment, surgically removed in a field hospital while he was awake. He never spoke of it.

"Sir?"

"The Germans." Driant gestured toward the eastern ridgeline, invisible in the pre-dawn gloom. "I have sent word to Headquarters. Three times in the past month. German activity. Artillery emplacements. Fresh troops. Supplies moving forward." He paused. "They ignore me."

"General Herr believes—"

"General Herr is a fool," Driant said, not unkindly. It was a statement of fact. "He has stripped the forts. Pulled back the guns. The line is hollow. And he does not believe the Germans will attack here."

"And you do, sir."

"I know they will." Driant turned to face him. His eyes—gray, tired, infinitely sad—met Moreau's. "I have been a soldier for forty years, Henri. I know when a storm is coming. I can feel it."

Moreau said nothing. There was nothing to say.

Driant reached into his coat and withdrew a sealed envelope. "This is for my wife. If I do not survive—"

"Sir, you must not—"

"Take it."

Moreau took the letter. It was heavier than it should have been.

"I wrote it last night," Driant said. "I have the strangest presentiment. Not fear. Not exactly. Just... certainty." He smiled faintly. "You will think me superstitious."

"No, sir."

"Good." Driant turned back to the parapet. "Make sure the men have hot coffee. If they are to die today, let them die warm."

---

### 07:10 — The Waiting

Caporal Marcel Renard crouched in the forward trench, hands wrapped around a tin cup of coffee that had long since gone cold. He didn't drink it. He just held it, feeling the metal through his gloves.

Around him, the men of the 59th Chasseurs waited.

There were fifteen of them in this section of trench: boys and veterans, farmers and clerks, men from Lille and Lyon and Marseille. Renard knew them all. He knew that Private Beaumont—red-haired, nineteen, from Paris—had never kissed a girl. He knew that Sergeant Dulac, thirty-five, from Bordeaux, had been a soldier when most of them were still in school. He knew that Corporal Gauthier kept a rosary in his left pocket and touched it before every barrage.

Renard knew that half of them would be dead by nightfall.

He didn't know which half.

"Renard."

He looked up. It was young Beaumont, pale and shivering. The boy's rifle was slung over his shoulder, barrel down to keep snow out. His hands were shaking.

"What is it?"

"Do you think... do you think they'll come today?"

Renard looked at him. Beaumont's eyes were wide, wet, terrified. A child's eyes.

"Yes," Renard said.

"How do you know?"

"Because it's quiet."

Beaumont swallowed. "I'm scared."

"Good. Fear keeps you alive." Renard took a sip of the cold coffee, grimaced, spat it into the mud. "Just do what I tell you. Stay low. Conserve your ammunition. If they come, you shoot until the rifle is empty, then you reload. You do not think. You do not run. You shoot. Understand?"

Beaumont nodded, unconvincing.

Renard reached into his pocket and withdrew a scrap of blue ribbon—faded, frayed, knotted at both ends. He held it up.

"You see this?"

"Yes."

"My daughter gave it to me. Céleste. She is seven years old. I have not seen her in two years." He tucked it back into his pocket. "I carry this because I have something to return to. You have a mother in Paris, yes?"

"Yes."

"Then you have something to return to. Remember that."

Beaumont nodded again, steadier this time.

"Good." Renard stood, brushing mud from his trousers. "Now check your rifle. Make sure the bolt is clean."

---

### 07:12 — The German Line

Across the ravine, in the German trenches, Leutnant Friedrich Steinbrecher stood with his men and waited for the order.

He was twenty-eight years old, a theology student from Baden, and he had been at war for eighteen months. He no longer believed in God. Or perhaps God no longer believed in him. It was difficult to say.

He checked his watch: 07:12.

Three minutes.

Behind him, stretching back along the ridgeline for twenty kilometers, were 1,220 guns. Heavy siege mortars, field howitzers, 77mm cannons, and—somewhere in the rear, he had heard—the monstrous 420mm "Big Berthas," railway guns that could hurl a shell the weight of a horse.

He had seen the ammunition dumps. Mountains of shells. Enough to erase a city.

All of it pointed at the forest in front of him.

"Herr Leutnant."

Steinbrecher turned. It was Gefreiter Hans Wolff, a bank clerk from Berlin, twenty-four years old, thin-faced, nervous. His eyes flicked toward the French lines, then back.

"Will we attack after the bombardment?"

"No," Steinbrecher said. "Not today. Today we only destroy."

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow we walk through the ruins."

Wolff nodded. He looked ill.

Steinbrecher took out his pocket watch again: 07:14.

One minute.

He wondered if the French knew. He wondered if they were standing in their trenches right now, looking east, waiting. He wondered if they had families. Wives. Children. He wondered if it mattered.

It did not matter.

He closed the watch.

"Take cover," he said.

---

## PART II: THE DRUM

### 07:15 — The World Ends

The sound came first.

Not the shells—those would come later. First, there was the sound of 1,220 guns firing simultaneously, a sound so vast it was not sound anymore but pressure, a physical force that struck the chest like a fist.

Driant heard it and knew.

Moreau heard it and dropped his coffee cup.

Renard heard it and pulled Beaumont down into the trench.

Then the sky fell.

---

### 07:16 — Trommelfeuer

The Germans called it *Trommelfeuer*: drumfire.

It was not a metaphor.

The shells fell in waves, overlapping, continuous, a drumbeat without pause. The sound merged into a single roar, oceanic, apocalyptic. The earth shook. The air became solid with concussion.

Moreau pressed himself against the trench wall, hands over his ears, mouth open to equalize the pressure. The world was ending. He was certain of it. The world was ending and he would die here, buried in mud, unmourned, forgotten.

A shell landed ten meters away. The explosion lifted the ground, bent the air, inverted reality. Moreau felt himself thrown sideways, weightless, suspended. Then he struck the ground and the breath left his body.

He lay in the mud, gasping, deaf.

Around him, the trench was disintegrating. Sandbags exploded into clouds of sand. Timber supports splintered. Men screamed but he could not hear them. He could see their mouths open, their faces contorted, but there was only the roar, the eternal roar.

He tried to stand. His legs would not obey. He crawled instead, pulling himself through the mud toward the dugout entrance. A shell landed behind him—close, too close. Heat washed over him. Shrapnel hissed past his head.

He fell into the dugout.

Inside, in the semi-darkness, six men were huddled against the walls. Their eyes were white, animal. One of them was praying. Another was weeping. A third—Corporal Gauthier—was touching his rosary, over and over, lips moving silently.

Moreau pulled himself into a corner and tried to breathe.

Outside, the bombardment continued.

---

### 08:00 — Forty-Five Minutes

Driant moved through the ruined trench like a ghost.

He was untouched. He did not know how. Men had died beside him—torn apart, buried, incinerated—but he walked unharmed. Perhaps God had preserved him. Perhaps God had simply overlooked him.

The trench no longer resembled a trench. It was a crater-field, a lunar landscape, a vision of the end of all things. Bodies lay half-buried in the mud. Some were intact. Most were not. The wounded screamed and screamed and no one could hear them.

Driant found a group of chasseurs sheltering in a shell hole. Ten men. No, nine—one was dead, sitting upright against the mud wall, eyes open, a small hole in his forehead.

"Colonel!" one of them shouted. His voice was distant, muffled, as though he were speaking underwater.

Driant's ears were bleeding. He could barely hear.

"Stay down!" he shouted back. "Do not leave the holes! Wait for the barrage to lift!"

"When will it lift?"

Driant looked at his watch. It had stopped. The crystal was cracked. He threw it away.

"I do not know."

---

### 09:00 — The Mathematics of Annihilation

In the first hour, the German guns fired 100,000 shells.

In the second hour, they fired 100,000 more.

The Bois des Caures ceased to exist. The forest became matchsticks. The trenches became ditches. The ditches became craters. The craters became graves.

By 10:00, the French communication lines were severed. The field telephones were dead. The runners sent to the rear did not return—killed by shrapnel, buried by explosions, or simply lost in the smoke and chaos.

The 56th and 59th Chasseurs were cut off.

Driant knew this. He had known it would happen. He had written about it in his reports, his warnings, his letters to Paris. He had told them: *The line is too thin. The guns are too few. The forts are empty. We cannot hold.*

No one had listened.

Now they would.

---

### 12:00 — Noon

The bombardment paused.

The silence was worse than the noise.

Moreau climbed out of the dugout, ears ringing, legs unsteady. The world was gray: gray smoke, gray mud, gray sky. The air smelled of sulfur and burning wood and something sweet and rotten.

Bodies.

He counted six in the immediate vicinity. Then he stopped counting.

"Sir."

He turned. It was Renard, blood-streaked, one sleeve torn, but alive. Behind him, Beaumont—pale, shaking, but alive.

"Casualties?" Moreau asked.

"Half the section. Maybe more. Dulac is rallying the survivors."

"The Germans?"

"Not yet."

"They will come."

"I know."

They looked east, toward the ridgeline. The smoke was thinning. Through the haze, they could see movement: gray shapes, hundreds of them, thousands, advancing in lines.

"Fix bayonets," Moreau said.

---

### 14:00 — The Assault Postponed

The German infantry reached the edge of the bombardment zone and stopped.

The officers checked their watches. The bombardment had lifted at 12:00. The assault was scheduled for 16:00—four hours to allow the smoke to clear, the artillery to adjust, the assault troops to move forward.

But the snow was falling again. Visibility was poor. The Crown Prince's staff, cautious, ordered a delay.

The attack would resume at 16:00.

The French, dazed and bleeding in their shell holes, did not know this.

They waited.

---

## PART III: THE FIRST DEAD

### 15:30 — Colonel Driant

Driant found Moreau in what remained of the command post: a crater with a few salvaged timbers for overhead cover.

"Henri."

Moreau stood, saluted. "Sir."

"How many men?"

"Perhaps three hundred. The 56th is nearly gone. The 59th still has two companies."

"Ammunition?"

"Enough for two hours of fighting. Maybe three."

Driant nodded. He looked older than he had this morning. His face was gray, hollow. His uniform was torn, mud-caked. But his eyes were steady.

"The Germans will attack at dusk," he said. "We will hold as long as we can. When we can hold no longer, we will withdraw to the secondary line."

"And if there is no secondary line?"

"Then we will die here."

Moreau said nothing.

Driant reached out and gripped his shoulder. "You are a good officer, Henri. You will survive this. I will not. Do you understand?"

"Sir—"

"Listen to me. When I am gone, you will take command of what remains. You will withdraw in good order. You will not let the men break. Do you understand?"

"Yes, sir."

"Good." Driant released him. He took a breath, steadied himself. "Now. Let us prepare."

---

### 16:00 — The Attack

The German barrage resumed at 16:00, but this time it was different: shorter, sharper, a rolling curtain of fire that crept forward across the French positions.

Behind it, the infantry advanced.

They came in waves: feldgrau-clad soldiers with rifles and stick grenades and flamethrowers, moving through the smoke, climbing over the craters, crossing the shattered forest.

The French opened fire.

Renard stood in the shell hole, rifle braced against his shoulder, and shot at the shapes moving through the smoke. Beside him, Beaumont was firing wildly, tears streaming down his face, screaming something Renard could not hear.

"Aim!" Renard shouted. "Aim, damn you!"

He shot a German soldier at thirty meters. The man fell, clutching his chest.

He shot another. And another.

The rifle jammed. He cleared it, loaded a fresh clip, fired again.

The Germans kept coming.

---

### 17:00 — The Ravine

The fighting became hand-to-hand.

Germans poured into the French positions, bayonets fixed, grenades exploding in the narrow trenches. The French fought back with rifles, knives, shovels, fists.

Moreau shot a German officer in the face at point-blank range. The man's head snapped back and he fell into the mud. Another German lunged at him with a bayonet. Moreau parried with his rifle, swung the butt into the man's jaw. Bone cracked. The man collapsed.

Around him, men screamed and died.

He saw Gauthier—the corporal with the rosary—grappling with a German soldier twice his size. The German drove a knife into Gauthier's stomach. Gauthier gasped, blood bubbling from his lips, and fell.

He saw Dulac, the veteran sergeant, shoot three Germans in quick succession, calm and methodical, as though he were on a rifle range.

He saw Beaumont—young, terrified Beaumont—standing in the open, firing his rifle until it was empty, then swinging it like a club, sobbing, until a bullet struck him in the chest and he crumpled.

Moreau screamed his name.

There was no answer.

---

### 18:00 — Darkness

By the time night fell, the Bois des Caures was lost.

The Germans had pushed the French back to the second line, then the third. The chasseurs fought for every meter, every shell hole, every shattered tree.

But they were too few, and the Germans were too many.

Driant organized the withdrawal. He moved from position to position, steady and calm, ordering men back, covering their retreat with rifle fire.

"Fall back to the ravine!" he shouted. "Fall back!"

The men obeyed.

Driant stayed.

---

### 18:30 — The Last of Colonel Driant

He stood at the edge of the ravine, rifle in hand, and fired at the advancing Germans.

Behind him, Moreau was shouting: "Sir! We must go! Sir!"

Driant did not answer. He fired until the rifle was empty, then drew his pistol.

A German soldier appeared through the smoke, twenty meters away. Driant shot him. Another came. He shot him too.

"Sir!"

Driant turned. "Go, Henri. Go now."

"I will not leave you!"

"That is an order."

Their eyes met. Moreau saw the truth there, the certainty.

"Go," Driant said.

Moreau went.

---

Driant turned back to the ravine. He could see them now: dozens of Germans, moving through the trees, surrounding him.

He raised his pistol.

A shot rang out.

He felt the impact—sharp, hot, in the center of his forehead.

The world tilted.

He fell.

His last thought was of his wife.

---

## EPILOGUE: FEBRUARY 22, 1916 — 06:00

Moreau stood in the reserve trench, two kilometers behind the Bois des Caures, and watched the sun rise.

Around him, the survivors of the 56th and 59th Chasseurs sat in the mud, silent, hollow-eyed. There were perhaps two hundred of them. There had been two thousand yesterday.

Renard was there, blood-crusted, one arm bandaged. Dulac was there, cleaning his rifle with mechanical precision. Beaumont was not there. Gauthier was not there.

Colonel Driant was not there.

Moreau opened his diary. His hands were shaking. He had not slept. He did not think he would ever sleep again.

He wrote:

> *February 21, 1916. 07:15.*
>
> *The world ended today.*
>
> *I do not know how to describe it. I do not know if words exist.*
>
> *One million shells. Nine hours. Then they came.*
>
> *Colonel Driant is dead. He died leading us. He died as he lived: without fear.*
>
> *I do not know if we will survive this. I do not know if survival is possible.*
>
> *But we will fight. Because there is nothing else to do.*

He closed the diary.

In the distance, the German guns began again.
