# THE SIEGE OF FORT VAUX
## June 1-7, 1916

*"The most heroic resistance of the Great War"*
— Gen. Robert Nivelle

---

## PROLOGUE: MAY 31, 1916 — THE LAST DAY OF WATER

Major Sylvain-Eugène Raynal stood in the main gallery of Fort Vaux and listened to the bombardment.

It had been going on for five days. Five days without pause. The Germans were using everything—150mm howitzers, 210mm mortars, flamethrowers, gas. The fort shook with each impact. Dust fell from the ceiling in perpetual streams. The electric lights had failed on day two. Now they worked by candlelight and the occasional flash of explosion.

Raynal was forty-nine years old, a career officer, methodical and unflappable. He had assumed command of Fort Vaux three weeks earlier, when the previous commander was wounded. He had not wanted the assignment. No one wanted Fort Vaux.

But he had accepted it. Because that was what officers did.

Now he was responsible for 600 men—infantry, machine gunners, engineers, medics, and a hundred wounded who had fled into the fort when the German assault began. 600 men in a concrete tomb, surrounded by an enemy division.

The water cistern had been hit on May 27. Four days ago. The reserve tank cracked. Ten thousand liters drained into the earth.

They had perhaps two hundred liters left.

In a fort with 600 men.

Raynal did the mathematics. It was simple and terrible.

Two hundred liters. Six hundred men.

One-third of a liter per man. Per day. If it lasted three days.

Less than a canteen. In June. In a sealed concrete fort where the temperature reached 40 degrees Celsius. Where men sweated through their uniforms. Where the wounded cried out for water, always water, *please God just water*.

There would be no resupply. The fort was cut off. The French had tried twice to break through with water carriers. Both attempts failed. Thirty men dead, no water delivered.

Raynal looked at the map on the table before him. Fort Vaux, a small polygon on the paper. Around it: German positions, marked in red. To the west: French lines, three kilometers away. Three kilometers that might as well be three thousand.

He folded the map.

"Gentlemen," he said to his officers, "we will hold as long as we can. And then we will hold longer."

---

## JUNE 1, 1916 — DAY ONE: THE ASSAULT BEGINS

### 04:00

The bombardment intensified.

Raynal, who had thought he understood what "intense" meant, learned he had been wrong. The Germans brought up every gun they had. The fort disappeared inside a storm of explosives. The sound was beyond human scale. Men's ears bled.

In the galleries, soldiers pressed themselves against the walls and prayed. Some wept. Some laughed—the high, brittle laughter of men who no longer control their minds. Some simply sat, staring at nothing, rocking back and forth.

Raynal moved through the fort, showing himself. Calm. Steady. As though bombardment were merely weather, to be endured without complaint.

"It will pass," he told them. "We have held before. We will hold again."

A lie. But a necessary one.

---

### 10:00

The bombardment lifted.

In the sudden silence—broken only by the ringing in their ears—the defenders knew what came next.

"To your posts!" Raynal ordered.

Men scrambled to the firing positions. Machine gunners checked their weapons. Infantry fixed bayonets.

Through the smoke, the Germans came.

---

### 10:15 — The First Wave

They came in masses—feldgrau-clad infantry, assault troops with grenades and flamethrowers, engineers with demolition charges. They came from all sides, surrounding the fort, probing for weakness.

The French opened fire.

Machine guns chattered. Rifles cracked. The Germans fell by the dozens, but more came. Always more.

The fort's exterior was a maze of galleries, tunnels, and firing positions. The Germans pushed into the outer works. Hand-to-hand fighting erupted in the corridors. Men fought with bayonets, knives, shovels, fists. The stone walls echoed with screams.

Raynal stood in the command post, receiving reports:

"West gallery holding. Heavy casualties."
"East entrance breached. We're falling back to the interior line."
"North machine gun nest destroyed. Flamethrower."
"South corridor—" Static. The telephone line was cut.

---

### 14:00

The Germans held the superstructure—the fort's exterior. The French held the interior galleries and the main citadel. A siege within a siege.

The Germans tried to force the interior gates. Grenades exploded in the narrow corridors. Men died in the darkness, unsure who they were killing.

At one point, a German flamethrower crew reached the inner gate. They fired. Liquid fire poured into the corridor. Men burned alive. The screaming was inhuman.

But the gate held. And French machine gunners—firing from murder holes above—killed the flamethrower crew.

The Germans withdrew.

---

### 18:00 — Water

A soldier came to Raynal. "Sir, the men are asking for water."

"Half rations," Raynal said. "One sip per man."

"Sir—"

"One sip."

The soldier saluted and left.

Later, Raynal stood in the water storage room and watched as men lined up with their canteens. Each man received a single mouthful. Some drank it immediately. Others held it in their mouths, savoring, trying to make it last.

A wounded man—both legs shattered—begged for water. The medic gave him a wet rag to suck on. There was nothing else to give.

Raynal calculated: At this rate, the water would last two days. Maybe three.

After that...

He did not finish the thought.

---

## JUNE 2, 1916 — DAY TWO: THIRST

### Morning

The temperature inside the fort rose. No ventilation. Six hundred men breathing, sweating, bleeding in sealed concrete chambers. The air was thick, foul, suffocating.

Men removed their tunics. They sat in their undershirts, gasping. Candles guttered in the thin air.

And the thirst began.

Not the ordinary thirst of a hot day. This was something else. This was the body consuming itself. Tongues swelling. Lips cracking. Throats closing.

Men stopped sweating. That was the sign: when you stopped sweating, your body was shutting down.

"Water," they said. "Please. Water."

There was none to give.

---

### 11:00 — The German Proposal

A German officer, under a white flag, approached the fort entrance (or what remained of it).

He shouted: "Frenchmen! You are surrounded. You have no water. You have no relief. Surrender with honor. Your bravery is recognized. You will be treated as prisoners of war, not criminals. You have my word."

From inside, silence.

Then Raynal's voice, calm and clear: "We will not surrender. Attack if you dare."

The German officer saluted—a gesture of respect—and withdrew.

The assault resumed.

---

### 14:00 — The Carrier Pigeon

Raynal wrote a message on a slip of paper:

> *"We are holding. Germans attacking from all sides. Citadel intact but superstructure lost. Water situation critical. Men dying of thirst. Request immediate relief. Vive la France. — Raynal"*

He called for the pigeon keeper—a young soldier named Privat.

"How many pigeons left?"

"One, sir."

One. They had started with twenty. Nineteen released, nineteen shot down by German marksmen or killed by gas.

One pigeon. Their last link to the outside world.

"This is the most important message you will ever send," Raynal said. "Make sure he survives."

Privat took the pigeon—a gray bird, battered, exhausted from the days in the fort. He attached the message to its leg. He whispered to it: "Fly well, little one. Everything depends on you."

He released it through a narrow slit in the fort wall.

The pigeon flew.

German soldiers saw it. They fired. Bullets cracked through the air.

The pigeon flew on.

It reached the French lines. It landed, wounded, in a communication trench. A soldier found it.

The message was delivered.

The pigeon—later named *Vaillant* (Valiant)—collapsed and died shortly after.

It was awarded the *Croix de Guerre*.

---

### 18:00 — The Reply

The French artillery fired in response—a barrage meant to signal: *We received your message. Hold on.*

But no relief force came. The Germans held the approaches. Any attempt to break through would be suicide.

Raynal understood. They were alone.

---

## JUNE 3, 1916 — DAY THREE: DELIRIUM

### Morning

The water was gone.

Not rationed. Not reduced. *Gone.*

Men sucked moisture from the stone walls. They licked condensation from metal pipes. They drank their own urine—bitter, burning, but liquid.

Some went mad. They clawed at the walls, screaming for water. They had to be restrained.

Others simply lay still, tongues black, waiting to die.

The wounded were the worst. They had lost blood. Their bodies needed water to replace it. Without water, they dried out like leather.

A young soldier—Beaumont, nineteen, gut wound—begged the medic: "Please. Please just a little water. Just a little."

The medic had nothing. He held the boy's hand and lied: "Soon. Relief is coming soon."

Beaumont died an hour later. Not from his wound. From thirst.

---

### 12:00 — The Last Telephone Line

The Germans had cut all communication lines. Except one. A single telephone cable, somehow intact, running from Fort Vaux to French headquarters three kilometers away.

Raynal picked up the receiver.

Static. Then a voice: "Vaux, this is HQ. Report."

"Raynal. Situation critical. No water for two days. Men dying of thirst. Wounded cannot be treated. Morale... holding, but barely. Germans control superstructure. We hold the citadel. Request immediate relief. Over."

Silence. Then: "Relief impossible. Germans have the approaches. You must hold. France is watching. Over."

"Understood. We will hold. Over."

"God be with you. Over and out."

The line went dead. The Germans had found the cable.

They were truly alone now.

---

### 16:00 — The Gas Attack

The Germans pumped phosgene gas into the fort's ventilation shafts.

Alarms rang. "Gas! Gas! Masks!"

Men fumbled for their masks in the darkness. Some couldn't find them. Some masks were broken. Some men were too weak to seal them properly.

The gas seeped through the corridors—invisible, odorless, deadly.

Men coughed. Choked. Drowned in their own lungs.

Raynal, masked, moved through the fort, checking positions. He found seven men dead in one corridor, masks half-on, faces blue.

There was no time for burial. No time for prayer. The bodies were dragged to a side chamber and the door sealed.

They would deal with the dead later. If there was a later.

---

## JUNE 4, 1916 — DAY FOUR: THE DECISION

### 03:00

Raynal did not sleep. He stood in the command post, surrounded by maps that no longer mattered, and faced the truth.

He had 600 men when the siege began. Now he had perhaps 400 functional. Two hundred casualties—killed, wounded, incapacitated by gas or thirst.

Of the 400 remaining, half were barely conscious. Dehydrated. Delirious. Unable to fight.

The Germans controlled 90% of the fort. The French held only the central citadel—a cluster of rooms and corridors, perhaps fifty meters square.

They had no water. No food (they had stopped eating—eating made you thirsty). No medical supplies. No ammunition reserves.

No hope of relief.

The mathematics were simple. They could hold for one more day. Maybe two, if the men found strength they did not possess.

And then?

Surrender or death.

Raynal was not a man prone to dramatics. He did not think in terms of glory or heroism. He thought in terms of duty and practicality.

His duty was to hold Fort Vaux as long as possible. To delay the Germans. To bleed them. To deny them victory for as long as he could.

But his duty was also to his men. To not spend their lives pointlessly. To recognize when further resistance was not heroism but murder.

When was that point?

He did not know.

---

### 08:00 — The German Commander

Through the telephone line (which the Germans had repaired for this purpose), a German voice:

"Major Raynal?"

"Speaking."

"This is Generalleutnant von Mudra, 50th Division. I wish to speak with you about terms."

"I do not surrender."

"I am not asking you to. I am asking you to listen."

Silence.

"Major, you have defended Fort Vaux with extraordinary courage. Your garrison has fought brilliantly. You have delayed my division for six days. You have inflicted hundreds of casualties. You have done your duty. No man could ask more.

"But Major, you have no water. You have no relief. You have no hope. Your men are dying not from German bullets but from thirst. You know this. I know this.

"I offer you honorable terms. Lay down your arms. March out with your officers. You will be treated as prisoners of war, with all due respect. Your wounded will receive medical care. Your men will receive water.

"There is no shame in this, Major. Only necessity."

Raynal said nothing for a long moment. Then: "I will consult with my officers."

"You have two hours."

The line went dead.

---

### 10:00 — The Council

Raynal gathered his surviving officers—eight men, gaunt, hollow-eyed, barely able to stand.

"Gentlemen. You have heard the German offer. I ask for your counsel."

Lieutenant Girard, twenty-four, said: "We cannot surrender. We are French soldiers. We do not surrender."

Captain Roy, forty, said: "We are also responsible for our men. To let them die of thirst when we can save them—is that duty, or pride?"

Lieutenant Buffet, gasping (gas exposure): "If we surrender, the Germans take Vaux. If we hold, we delay them. Every hour we hold is one more hour for France to prepare."

"And if we hold until every man is dead?" Roy asked. "What is the purpose?"

Silence.

Raynal said: "Gentlemen, I will not order you to die of thirst. But I will not surrender while we can still fight. We will hold for one more day. If relief has not come by tomorrow evening, I will negotiate terms. Does anyone object?"

No one objected.

"Then we hold."

---

### 14:00 — The Last Defense

The Germans attacked again. A final push, trying to finish the siege.

They brought flamethrowers into the citadel. Fire poured through the corridors. Men burned. The stone walls glowed red-hot.

French defenders, too weak to run, fired their rifles point-blank. Some fought with bayonets. Some threw grenades and were caught in their own explosions.

At one point, a German soldier and a French soldier grappled in a side corridor. Both were exhausted. Both were dying of thirst. They clawed at each other feebly, too weak to inflict harm, until both collapsed.

They lay beside each other, breathing heavily.

The German said, in broken French: "Water?"

The Frenchman shook his head. "Non."

They lay there in the darkness, enemies who were also brothers in suffering, until the German died.

---

## JUNE 5, 1916 — DAY FIVE: THE WAIT

The day passed in a haze. Men lay where they had fallen, too weak to move.

Some prayed. Some hallucinated—saw rivers, lakes, oceans. Some simply stared.

Raynal moved through the fort, checking positions. Fewer men answered each time.

A medic approached him. "Sir, we have a problem."

"Another one?"

"The wounded. They're drinking... they're drinking the iodine. The disinfectant."

Raynal closed his eyes. Men were so desperate for liquid they were drinking antiseptic. It would poison them, but they were dying anyway.

"Let them," he said. "It's all we have."

---

## JUNE 6, 1916 — DAY SIX: THE COLLAPSE

### 10:00

A runner—barely able to walk—came to Raynal.

"Sir. The men. They're... they're giving up. They're lying down. They won't move."

Raynal went to see.

In the main chamber, dozens of men lay on the floor. Eyes open. Breathing. But motionless.

"On your feet!" Raynal ordered.

No one moved.

"Soldiers! On your feet!"

A few stirred. Most did not.

One man—a sergeant, name unknown—looked at Raynal and said, in a cracked whisper: "I'm sorry, sir. I can't. I just can't."

Raynal understood. These men had reached the limit of human endurance. They had nothing left.

He could order them. He could threaten them. It would not matter.

They were broken.

---

### 14:00 — The Decision

Raynal returned to the command post. He sat at the table—alone, in the darkness, surrounded by the smell of death and excrement and despair.

He thought of his duty. He thought of France. He thought of the men who had trusted him.

He thought of the sergeant's words: *I can't. I just can't.*

And he realized: neither could he.

Not because he was weak. But because there was nothing left to hold with. No men capable of fighting. No water. No hope.

He had done everything a commander could do. His men had done everything soldiers could do.

It was enough. It had to be enough.

He picked up the telephone.

"Connect me to the German command."

---

### 16:00 — The Negotiation

"Generalleutnant von Mudra."

"This is Major Raynal. I am prepared to discuss terms."

A pause. Then: "You have my respect, Major. State your terms."

"My men will surrender. We will march out under arms. We will stack arms outside the fort, but we will not be disarmed by your soldiers. Officers will keep their sidearms and swords. Wounded will be treated immediately. These are my conditions."

Another pause.

"Agreed. You have my word. Assemble your men at 18:00. We will receive you with honor."

"Understood."

Raynal hung up. He sat for a moment, alone in the dark.

Then he stood. There was work to do.

---

## JUNE 7, 1916 — 18:00 — THE SURRENDER

The gate of Fort Vaux opened.

Major Sylvain-Eugène Raynal stepped out first, alone. He wore his dress uniform (somehow still intact), kept clean for this moment. He carried his sword.

Behind him, the survivors of Fort Vaux: four hundred men, gaunt, filthy, barely able to walk. They carried their rifles. They marched in formation, or as close to formation as men dying of thirst could manage.

The Germans stood in ranks, silent.

As Raynal approached, Generalleutnant von Mudra stepped forward. He saluted.

Raynal returned the salute.

"Major Raynal," von Mudra said, "you and your men have defended Fort Vaux with extraordinary courage. You have my respect and the respect of the German Army. I regret that we meet under these circumstances."

"As do I," Raynal said.

"You may keep your sword."

Raynal hesitated. To keep one's sword after surrender was an honor reserved for the most gallant defenders. It meant: *You were not defeated. You were simply overwhelmed by circumstances beyond your control.*

"Thank you," Raynal said.

He removed his sword belt and handed it—and the sword—to his adjutant. "Keep this safe. We will need it when France reclaims this fort."

The French soldiers stacked their rifles in neat pyramids. Then, one by one, they were led to water stations the Germans had prepared.

The first man to drink—Sergeant Dulac, somehow still alive—took a single mouthful, choked, and vomited. His body had forgotten how to process water.

But he drank again. And again.

Around him, men wept with relief.

## EPILOGUE

Fort Vaux was held by the Germans for five months.

On November 2, 1916, the Germans evacuated it during the French counteroffensive. When French forces reoccupied the fort, they found it empty except for one thing:

A message, written in German on the wall:

> *"Here fought the bravest of the brave."*

Major Raynal spent the rest of the war in a German prisoner camp. He was repatriated in 1918. He was awarded the Légion d'honneur and promoted to colonel.

He never spoke publicly about Fort Vaux. When asked, he said only: "We did our duty."

He died in 1939, three months before the Second World War began.

## HISTORICAL NOTES

This narrative is based on historical accounts of the Siege of Fort Vaux, including:
- Major Raynal's official report (written in captivity)
- German after-action reports
- Survivor testimonies
- Archaeological evidence from the fort

Key facts:
- The water cistern was destroyed on May 27, 1916
- The garrison endured 4 days without water (June 3-7)
- The carrier pigeon *Vaillant* successfully delivered the message on June 4
- Raynal did keep his sword upon surrender (honor of the defeated)
- The siege is considered one of the most heroic defenses of WWI
- Fort Vaux was retaken by France, November 2, 1916

**Game Integration**:
- **Standalone Mission**: "The Siege of Fort Vaux" (7-day compressed narrative)
- **Gameplay Mechanics**:
  - Water meter (decreases daily, affects stamina, vision, accuracy)
  - Defense gameplay (hold corridors, repel assaults, manage limited ammunition)
  - Moral choices (who gets water rations? surrender vs. resist?)
  - Fog of war (darkness, smoke, confusion)
  - Rescue pigeon mini-mission (player releases Vaillant, protects him from fire)
- **Cutscenes**:
  - Raynal's decision to hold (day 4)
  - Raynal's decision to surrender (day 6)

- **NPCs**: Raynal (commander, steady voice), Sergeant Dulac (survivor), pigeon keeper Privat
